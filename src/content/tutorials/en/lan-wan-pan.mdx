PAN, LAN, MAN, WAN: these acronyms show up in every job ad, every course and every piece of documentation. They
classify networks by **geographic reach**, and behind the distance lie very concrete differences: who owns the
cables, which technologies are used, what bandwidth you get, and how much it costs.

| Type | Reach | Typical technologies | Common bandwidth | Who owns the links |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PAN** (*Personal Area Network*) | A few metres, around a person | Bluetooth, USB, NFC | A few kb/s to a few Mb/s | You |
| **LAN** (*Local Area Network*) | A building, a floor | Ethernet, Wi-Fi | 1 to 100 Gb/s | The company |
| **CAN** (*Campus Area Network*) | Several nearby buildings | Fibre between buildings | 10 to 100 Gb/s | The company |
| **MAN** (*Metropolitan Area Network*) | A city, a metropolitan area | Dark fibre, metro Ethernet | 1 to 100 Gb/s | A carrier, or a local authority |
| **WAN** (*Wide Area Network*) | Cities, countries, continents | MPLS, Internet + VPN, SD-WAN | A few Mb/s to 10 Gb/s | One or more carriers |

## The PAN: around you

The personal area network connects one person's devices: Bluetooth headphones to the phone, a smartwatch, a
wireless keyboard. Short range, low power, little bandwidth. For a network engineer, the PAN matters mostly for
**security**: a badly configured Bluetooth device is a way into a workstation.

## The LAN: the network you manage

The local area network is that of a building or a site: workstations, printers, Wi-Fi access points, local
servers, connected by **switches** over **Ethernet** (see [Ethernet and MAC addresses](/en/courses/fundamentals/ethernet)).
It is a network administrator's core business:

- **the company owns everything**: cables, switches, access points. It can change them whenever it wants;
- **bandwidth is plentiful and cheap**: 1 Gb/s per workstation, 10 to 100 Gb/s between switches;
- **latency is very low**: under a millisecond;
- it is split into **VLANs** to separate uses (see [VLANs](/en/courses/fundamentals/vlan)).

The **WLAN** (*Wireless LAN*) is simply the wireless part of the LAN: Wi-Fi.

## The CAN and the MAN: between buildings

When a company occupies several buildings, on a university campus, an industrial site or a hospital, the LANs
are linked by **optical fibre** laid between the buildings: a campus network (CAN), still owned by the company.

At city scale, you can no longer lay your own cables under the streets. So you rent:

- **dark fibre**: a bare fibre, with no equipment, that you "light" with your own devices;
- a **metro Ethernet** service: the carrier provides an Ethernet link between two sites, like a very long cable.

That is the metropolitan area network (MAN). Local authorities also build them to connect their schools, town
halls and hospitals.

## The WAN: between cities and countries

The wide area network connects distant sites: headquarters in Paris, a branch in Lyon, a plant in Poland. Here,
the company **owns nothing**: it buys services from carriers. Everything changes:

- **bandwidth is expensive**: a few hundred Mb/s cost far more than 10 Gb/s in a LAN;
- **latency grows with distance**: light travels about 1,000 km in 5 ms in fibre, and a Paris to New York round
  trip takes about 75 ms;
- **you depend on a third party**: its service quality, its intervention times, its outages.

WAN technologies:

| Technology | Principle | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| **Leased line** | A dedicated point-to-point link between two sites | Historical, still used for critical needs |
| **MPLS** | A private network built by the carrier, connecting all the company's sites (see [MPLS](/en/tutorials/mpls)) | Large companies, for twenty years |
| **Internet + VPN** | Each site has Internet access, and an encrypted tunnel connects the sites (see [IPsec VPN](/en/courses/security/ipsec-vpn)) | Small organisations, and as a backup |
| **SD-WAN** | Central control that combines several links (MPLS, Internet, 4G/5G) and picks the best one for each application | The current trend |

**The Internet** itself is the largest WAN: a network of networks, connecting tens of thousands of operators (see
[How the Internet works on a global scale](/en/tutorials/internet-wan)).

<Callout type="field">
In a new-site project, it is almost always the **WAN** that sets the schedule. The LAN can be ordered and
configured in a few weeks; a carrier link often takes two to three months, sometimes more if civil works are
needed to bring the fibre in. Order the WAN link (and a backup link from another carrier) as soon as the site's
address is known, and plan temporary 4G/5G access.
</Callout>

## Other acronyms

Some "AN" acronyms are not about reach:

- **WLAN**: the wireless LAN (Wi-Fi);
- **VLAN** (*Virtual LAN*): a **logical** LAN, carved out of a physical LAN, unrelated to distance;
- **SAN** (*Storage Area Network*): the network dedicated to storage in a data center (Fibre Channel, iSCSI),
  separate from the server network;
- **SD-WAN** and **SD-LAN**: WANs or LANs driven by a central controller (*Software-Defined*).

## Key takeaways

- Network types are classified by **reach**: **PAN** (a person), **LAN** (a building), **CAN** (a campus), **MAN**
  (a city), **WAN** (countries).
- The wider the network, the less the company owns the links: **the LAN is yours, the WAN is rented** from carriers.
- LAN: plentiful bandwidth, minimal latency. WAN: expensive bandwidth, distance-bound latency, carrier dependency.
- WAN technologies: leased line, **MPLS**, **Internet + VPN**, **SD-WAN**.
- WLAN, VLAN and SAN do not describe a distance, but a **use** or a **logical** split.
