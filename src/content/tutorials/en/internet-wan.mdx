When you open a web page hosted on the other side of the world, the answer arrives in a fraction of a second.
Between your home router and the server, the packet crossed a dozen networks belonging to different companies,
often competitors, in several countries, sometimes at the bottom of the ocean. Nobody runs the Internet as a
whole: it works because tens of thousands of organisations have **agreements** with each other, and a common
**protocol** to announce their routes.

## A network of networks

The Internet is not one network: it is the **interconnection** of more than 70,000 independent networks, the
**autonomous systems** (<Term id="as" />). Each has a number (ASN) and runs its own routers and its own policy:

| AS type | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Access providers** | Your Internet provider, mobile operators | Connecting homes and businesses |
| **Transit providers** | Large international carriers | Carrying other networks' traffic, from one continent to another |
| **Content providers and clouds** | Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Netflix | Hosting services, as close to users as possible |
| **Companies and institutions** | Universities, banks, governments | Having their own addresses and several providers |

IP addresses and AS numbers are allocated by five **regional registries** (RIRs), under IANA's authority: **RIPE
NCC** for Europe and the Middle East, ARIN for North America, APNIC for Asia-Pacific, LACNIC for Latin America and
AFRINIC for Africa. Their databases are public: you can find who owns any address.

- **Transit**: a network pays a transit provider to reach the **whole** Internet. The provider announces every route
  in the world to it and carries its traffic everywhere. The largest providers, called *Tier 1*, buy transit from
  nobody: they exchange traffic among themselves.
- **Peering**: two networks exchange the traffic that concerns them **directly**, without an intermediary, most
  often without payment. An access provider and a video provider have every reason to do so: traffic is faster,
  and neither pays transit for it.

### Exchange points

Building a physical link with every network you want to peer with would cost too much. **Internet eXchange Points**
(IXPs) solve the problem: a large switched network, in a few data centers of a city, which hundreds of networks
connect to once. Each can then set up peering sessions with all the others. The largest are in Frankfurt (DE-CIX),
Amsterdam (AMS-IX) and London (LINX); in France, France-IX in Paris and Marseille.

## Submarine cables

Between continents, almost all Internet traffic travels through **fibre optic cables laid on the ocean floor**:
more than 500 cables, carrying most intercontinental traffic, far ahead of satellites. A modern cable carries
hundreds of terabits per second over a few fibre pairs, with repeaters every 50 to 100 km to amplify the signal.

They come ashore at **landing stations**, then reach data centers. **Marseille** is one of the world's main hubs:
around fifteen cables connect Europe to Africa, the Middle East and Asia there. Cables are built by carrier
consortiums and, more and more, by the content giants themselves. They are sometimes cut (ship anchors,
earthquakes): traffic then goes through the other cables, more slowly, while a cable ship repairs it.

## BGP, the Internet's glue

How does each network know how to reach the 70,000 others? Through <Term id="bgp" />: each AS announces to its
neighbours the prefixes it owns, and relays those it learned, according to its business agreements. A carrier
router thus knows more than a million IPv4 and IPv6 prefixes. The [Dynamic routing](/en/courses/routing) course
explains BGP in detail.

BGP relies on **trust**: an AS can announce, by mistake or malice, addresses that are not its own. In 2008, a
Pakistani operator wanting to block YouTube in its country announced YouTube's addresses to the whole world, and
made YouTube unreachable for two hours. In 2021, Facebook withdrew its own announcements by mistake: its services
vanished from the Internet for six hours. Operators now deploy <Term id="rpki" />, which lets them check that an AS
is entitled to announce a prefix.

What happens when you open `www.example.com` from home?

1. Your router asks your provider's **DNS** for the site's address (see
   [DDI: DNS, DHCP and IPAM](/en/courses/operations/ddi)).
2. The packet goes into your access provider's network, which looks up its BGP table.
3. If the hosting provider is one of its **peering** partners, the packet goes directly through an exchange point or
   a private link; otherwise, it goes to a **transit provider**.
4. The packet reaches the hosting provider's network, then the server.

For large services, the server is often **very close to you**: video providers and content delivery networks
(CDNs) install cache servers directly inside access providers' networks. Your video then crosses no ocean.

## Observing it from home

These tools work from any Linux, macOS or WSL machine connected to the Internet.

**1. The steps of the path.** `mtr` combines `traceroute` and `ping`: it shows each router crossed, with latency and
loss at each hop.

```bash
mtr -rwzc 10 one.one.one.one
```

The `-z` option shows the AS number of each hop: you see the packet leave your provider's network for
Cloudflare's. Compare with a distant destination, for example a service hosted in Australia: latency jumps when
crossing the ocean.

**2. Who owns an address.** Team Cymru's public service gives the AS of an IP address:

```bash
whois -h whois.cymru.com " -v 8.8.8.8"
```

You get the AS number, the announced prefix, the country and the organisation name. Try it with your own public
address.

**3. A network's profile.** On **PeeringDB** (`www.peeringdb.com`), look up your access provider: you see which
exchange points it connects to, and its peering policy. On **RIPEstat** (`stat.ripe.net`), look up an address or an
AS: announced prefixes, BGP neighbours, history, and RPKI validity.

## Key takeaways

- The Internet is a **network of networks**: more than 70,000 independent **autonomous systems**, with addresses
  allocated by five **regional registries**.
- **Transit** (paid, to the whole Internet) and **peering** (direct, often free); **exchange points** make peering
  possible at scale.
- **Submarine cables** carry most traffic between continents; Marseille is one of the world's major hubs.
- **BGP** ties it all together, based on trust, now strengthened by **RPKI**.
- Major content is served **close to you**, by caches installed inside access providers.
- `mtr`, `whois`, PeeringDB and RIPEstat let you see all this from home.
