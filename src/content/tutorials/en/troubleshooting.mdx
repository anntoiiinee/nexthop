"The Internet is down." "The app is slow." "I can't log in." Sooner or later, that message arrives, often at the
worst moment, with someone waiting. The temptation is to try everything at once: reboot, change a rule, swap a
cable. It is the best way to lose an hour, or to add a second outage to the first one. This tutorial offers a
**method**: slower on the surface, much more effective in practice.

## First, don't panic

The first minutes matter more than the next ones, and not for the reasons you might think. Before typing a
command:

1. **Breathe.** A network outage almost never needs an answer within the second. A wrong move under pressure
   does.
2. **Note the time** the problem was reported, and open a notes file. You will write down every check and every
   action in it, with the time. These notes help the troubleshooting ("what have I already tested?"), then the
   report.
3. **Let people know**: a short message to your manager or in the team channel ("incident in progress on this
   scope, I'm looking into it"). It stops three people from chasing the same thing separately, or a colleague
   from making a change at the same time as you.
4. **Don't touch anything until you understand.** Rebooting a device often erases the evidence: in-memory logs,
   ARP table, error counters, session state.

<Callout type="warning">
The golden rule of troubleshooting: **one change at a time**, and check its effect before the next one. If you
change three things and it works again, you will never know which one fixed it, nor whether the other two created
a new problem. And if it still doesn't work, undo the change before trying another one.
</Callout>

## Defining the problem

"It doesn't work" is not a problem, it is a symptom. A few questions, asked to the person reporting the incident,
narrow the search enormously:

| Question | What the answer tells you |
|---|---|
| **Who** is affected? One person, an office, a floor, a site, everyone? | The smaller the scope, the closer the cause is to the user: their computer, their cable, their switch port. If everyone is affected, look at the network core, the Internet access or a central service (DNS, DHCP). |
| **What** doesn't work? Everything, or a single application? | If everything is down, look at the lower layers (cable, VLAN, address, gateway). If a single application is affected, the basic network works: look at DNS, the firewall, the application itself. |
| **Since when**? A precise time? | A precise time lets you cross-check with logs, monitoring and changes. |
| **What changed?** | Often the most useful question. A planned change, an update, an office move, a new cable: most outages follow a change. |
| Is it **permanent or intermittent**? | An intermittent issue points to congestion, packet loss, a loop or an MTU problem. A clean outage points to a configuration or a failed device. |
| Is it **reproducible**? | If you can trigger the problem at will, you will be able to check it is really fixed. |

<Callout type="tip">
Restate the problem in one precise sentence before starting: "Since 9:40, computers on the 2nd floor can no longer
reach the intranet, while the Internet works." That sentence already holds half the diagnosis: the problem is
recent, localized, and affects an internal destination but not Internet access.
</Callout>

## First checks, in order

A network is a stack of layers (see the [OSI model](/en/courses/fundamentals/osi)): each needs the one below to
work. Checking in order, from the bottom up, avoids hunting for a DNS problem on an unplugged cable.

| # | Check | What to look at |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Physical link** | Is the cable plugged in, the LED on, the Wi-Fi connected? On the switch side, is the port `up`? Are the error counters (CRC, *input errors*) increasing? |
| 2 | **VLAN** | Is the port in the right VLAN? Does the machine show up in the switch's MAC table, on the right port? |
| 3 | **IP address** | Does the machine have an address, with the right mask? An address in `169.254.x.x` means no DHCP server answered. |
| 4 | **Gateway** | Is the default gateway the right one, and does it answer ping? Is its MAC address in the machine's ARP table? |
| 5 | **Routing** | Does a remote address answer ping (an IP, not a name)? Where does the path stop, according to `traceroute`? |
| 6 | **DNS** | Does the name resolve, and to the right address? Is the configured DNS server reachable? |
| 7 | **Port** | Is the service listening, and is the port open end to end (firewall, security group, local filtering)? |
| 8 | **Application** | If all of the above works, the network has done its job: certificate, authentication, the application itself. |

### The matching commands

| Check | Windows | Linux / macOS | Arista EOS / Cisco IOS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Link and counters | `Get-NetAdapter` | `ip link`, `ethtool eth0` | `show interfaces status`, `show interfaces counters errors` |
| VLAN and MAC table | | | `show vlan`, `show mac address-table` |
| IP address | `ipconfig /all` | `ip addr` | `show ip interface brief` |
| Gateway and ARP | `ping <gateway>`, `arp -a` | `ip route`, `ip neigh` | `show ip arp` |
| Routing | `tracert <ip>` | `traceroute <ip>`, `mtr <ip>` | `show ip route <ip>`, `traceroute <ip>` |
| DNS | `nslookup <name>` | `dig <name>` | |
| Port | `Test-NetConnection <ip> -Port 443` | `nc -vz <ip> 443` | |

<Callout type="note">
A failed ping doesn't prove the machine is down: many firewalls, and Windows by default, block ICMP. Conversely, a
ping that answers only proves the network layer works, not that the application is reachable. For a service, test
the port itself (`Test-NetConnection`, `nc`, or `curl`).
</Callout>

## Choosing a method

Checking every layer in order is safe, but not always the fastest. Three approaches, depending on the situation:

| Method | Principle | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Bottom up** | From the cable to the application, in the order of the table above | When you know nothing, or for a problem affecting a single computer |
| **Top down** | From the application down to the cable | When a single application is affected and everything else works |
| **Divide and conquer** | Start in the middle, with a ping to the gateway then to the destination, and go down or up depending on the result | The most effective in most cases: one test rules out half the hypotheses |

The most telling method for a network engineer is to **follow the path** of the packet, device by device,
checking at each step that it arrives and leaves:

At each point, a traffic capture (`tcpdump` on Linux, Wireshark, or a firewall's built-in capture) removes the
doubt: if the packet arrives but the reply never comes back, the problem is beyond that point, or on the return
path.

## The classic traps

Some outages come back so often that they deserve an early check:

- **DNS.** The application no longer answers, but the server's IP address answers ping? It is very often name
  resolution: wrong DNS server, changed record, stale cache. Hence the admins' saying: "it's always DNS".
- **MTU.** Pings and small pages go through, but large transfers hang, especially through a VPN or a tunnel. It is
  the signature of an MTU problem, detailed in the [TCP and UDP](/en/courses/fundamentals/tcp-udp) chapter. The
  [MTU calculator](/en/tools/mtu-calculator) gives the right values for each encapsulation.
- **Asymmetric routing.** The way out goes through one firewall, the way back through another path: the firewall
  only sees one direction of the connection and drops it. Ping may work while TCP fails.
- **IP address conflict.** Two machines with the same address: the connection works, drops, then comes back,
  depending on which machine last answered ARP.
- **Physical errors.** A damaged cable or a tired optic cause small but constant losses: everything is slow, nothing
  is down. The interface error counters show it.
- **Layer 2 loop.** A whole VLAN becomes unusable at once, LEDs blink frantically, switch CPUs saturate. Look for a
  cable plugged between two ports of the same switch, and check [Spanning Tree](/en/courses/fundamentals/stp).
- **Time.** A clock a few minutes off breaks Kerberos authentication, certificate validation or one-time tokens.
  Check NTP.
- **Expired certificate.** The network works perfectly, but the browser refuses the connection: it is a date, not
  a network outage.

<Callout type="field">
The network is often blamed first, because it is the part people see the least. Your job is not only to fix things:
it is also to **prove** where the problem is. "The packet reaches the server's interface, and the server doesn't
answer" is a sentence that saves everyone a lot of time, provided you have the capture to show it.
</Callout>

## During and after the incident

- **Communicate regularly**, even to say there is nothing new: an update every 30 minutes reassures people, and
  stops you from being interrupted constantly.
- **If a change is involved, roll back first**, look for the why afterwards. Restoring service comes before
  understanding.
- **Know when to escalate.** After a reasonable time with no lead, asking a colleague, the carrier or the vendor's
  support for help is not a failure. Your notes will save them precious time.
- **Find the root cause.** "The port was disabled" is an observation. "The port was disabled by broadcast storm
  control, triggered by a loop created by a cable plugged in twice" is a cause, and it says what to fix so it
  doesn't happen again.
- **Write a short report**: timeline, impact, cause, corrective actions. Without looking for someone to blame: the
  goal is to improve the system (monitoring, procedure, configuration), not to point fingers.

## Key takeaways

- **Don't panic**: note the time, let people know, reboot nothing before you understand.
- **Define the problem**: who, what, since when, and above all **what changed**.
- **Check in order**: link, VLAN, IP address, gateway, routing, DNS, port, application.
- **A method**: bottom up, top down, or divide and conquer; and follow the packet's path.
- **One change at a time**, write everything down, roll back if a change is involved.
- After the incident: **root cause** and report, so the same outage doesn't come back.
