**CloudVision** is Arista's network management platform: inventory, continuous telemetry, configuration and
upgrade management, compliance, all from a single console. It comes in two versions: **CloudVision Portal**
(CVP), installed on your own servers, and **CloudVision as-a-Service** (CVaaS), hosted by Arista. This tutorial
covers the architecture, installing CVP, onboarding switches and the first configuration changes.

CloudVision is a licensed product: no lab here. Arista offers CVaaS trials, and the onboarding principles are the
same in both versions.

## What CloudVision does

| Feature | What it brings |
|---|---|
| **Inventory** | Every switch, its model, its EOS version, its optics |
| **Telemetry** | Each switch's complete state, received continuously: interfaces, routes, MAC tables, events, with a history you can browse through time |
| **Provisioning** | Each switch's configuration, built from reusable blocks or templates, and applied through **change controls** |
| **Images** | EOS upgrades on groups of switches, with pre and post checks |
| **Compliance** | Detecting drift between intended and running configuration, and known vulnerabilities |

Telemetry is CloudVision's great strength: through the **TerminAttr** agent, each switch continuously streams its
whole internal state (its *Sysdb* database), and CloudVision keeps it. You can go back in time and see a switch's
routing table or MAC tables as they were when an incident happened.

## On premises or as a service

| | CVP (on premises) | CVaaS |
|---|---|---|
| **Where** | Your servers: virtual machines (VMware ESXi, KVM) or Arista appliances | Arista's cloud, in the region of your choice |
| **Operations** | Installation, backups, upgrades, monitoring: your job | Handled by Arista; new features available earlier |
| **Switch onboarding** | To your CVP nodes' addresses, port 9910 | To the CVaaS region's address, over HTTPS (443) |
| **For whom** | Networks isolated from the Internet, regulatory constraints | Most other cases |

## Installing CVP

### One node or a cluster

CVP installs on **a single node** (lab, small networks) or as a **three-node cluster**, which tolerates losing one
of them. In production, choose the cluster. The resources required (CPU, memory, disk) are significant and depend
on the version and the number of switches: several dozen cores and GB of memory per node. Refer to your version's
installation guide, and follow it: an undersized CVP is slow, and falls behind on telemetry.

### Before starting

CVP is demanding about its environment. Prepare:

- **a fixed IP address** per node, and a virtual address for the cluster if you use one;
- **forward and reverse DNS records** for each node: their absence is the first cause of installation failure;
- **a reachable NTP server**: cluster nodes and switches must share the same time;
- **open flows** between switches and CVP (port 9910 for TerminAttr, 443 for the interface and API), and between
  cluster nodes.

### The steps

1. **Deploy the image**: the OVA on VMware, or the KVM image, with the planned resources.
2. **Run the configuration**: on the virtual machine's console, log in with the `cvpadmin` account. An interactive
   wizard asks for the mode (single node or cluster), then each node's addresses, mask, gateway, DNS and NTP servers
   and name.
3. **Wait for the services to start**, several tens of minutes on the first run, and check it:

```bash title="On a CVP node"
cvpi status all
```

Every component must be `RUNNING`. The same `cvpi` commands stop and start services (`cvpi stop all`,
`cvpi start all`) and run backups (`cvpi backup cvp`).

4. **Log in to the web interface**, over HTTPS on the node's address, and create the administration accounts. In
   production, authentication goes through TACACS+ or RADIUS, as for the switches.

## Onboarding switches

### With TerminAttr

Each switch runs an agent, **TerminAttr**, which opens a connection to CloudVision and streams its telemetry.
Authentication uses a **token** generated in CloudVision (device onboarding menu), copied to the switch.

```text title="Arista EOS: onboarding to an on-premises CVP cluster"
daemon TerminAttr
   exec /usr/bin/TerminAttr -cvaddr=192.0.2.51:9910,192.0.2.52:9910,192.0.2.53:9910 -cvauth=token,/tmp/token -cvvrf=MGMT -taillogs
   no shutdown
```

- `-cvaddr`: the CVP nodes' addresses (the three nodes of a cluster);
- `-cvauth=token,/tmp/token`: the file holding the onboarding token;
- `-cvvrf=MGMT`: the VRF used to reach CVP, usually the management VRF (see
  [Management and out-of-band access](/en/courses/operations/oob));
- `-taillogs`: also send the switch's logs.

For CVaaS, `-cvaddr` points to your region's address over HTTPS (port 443) and authentication uses
`-cvauth=token-secure,<file>`; CloudVision shows the exact line to copy when generating the token.

```text title="Verify on the switch"
show daemon TerminAttr
```

The agent must be running. If something is wrong, its logs are in `/var/log/agents/` (from `bash`, `TerminAttr-*`
files): token rejected, address unreachable, clock drift.

The switch then shows up in CloudVision's inventory, and its telemetry starts flowing.

### With ZTP

For a new, unconfigured switch, CloudVision can also act as a **Zero Touch Provisioning** server: the switch boots,
gets an address over DHCP along with CVP's bootstrap script address (option 67), downloads the script, and
registers on its own. All that is left is to assign its configuration and image from CloudVision. It is the ideal
method to deploy a new fabric (see [DDI: DNS, DHCP and IPAM](/en/courses/operations/ddi) for DHCP options).

## Changing the configuration

CloudVision offers two approaches, which coexist depending on the version.

**Provisioning with configlets** (the historical approach): a switch's configuration is the assembly of
**configlets**, reusable configuration blocks (an "NTP" configlet shared by all, a "VLAN" configlet per site, a
configlet specific to the switch). They are assigned to containers (groups of switches) or to a switch, and
CloudVision computes the complete configuration.

**Studios** (the current approach): you describe the intent (the fabric, VLANs, services) in forms and templates,
and CloudVision generates the configurations. Changes are prepared in a **workspace**, which shows the differences
before submitting them.

Either way, nothing is applied directly: each change becomes a task, grouped into a **change control**. It is
reviewed, approved (by another person if you wish), then executed, switch by switch or in parallel, with rollback
available.

<Callout type="field">
The classic trap of the first months with CloudVision: changes made directly in CLI on a switch, "just to
troubleshoot". CloudVision detects the drift, and at the next change control on that switch, offers to restore its
own configuration, silently erasing the fix. Set a clear rule: either every change goes through CloudVision, or
every CLI fix is reported in CloudVision the same day.
</Callout>

## Automating around CloudVision

- **API**: CloudVision exposes a REST API and gRPC APIs (*Resource APIs*), for inventory, telemetry and
  provisioning.
- **Ansible**: the `arista.cvp` collection drives configlets, containers and change controls.
- **AVD**: [Arista AVD](/en/courses/automation/avd) generates a fabric's configurations from a data model, and can deploy them **through
  CloudVision**, to benefit from its change controls and telemetry.

## Key takeaways

- **CloudVision** manages an Arista fleet: inventory, continuous and historised **telemetry**, provisioning, images,
  compliance. On premises (**CVP**) or as **CVaaS**.
- CVP: one node or a **three-node cluster**, with a carefully prepared environment (forward and reverse DNS, NTP,
  open flows). `cvpi status all` to check.
- Switches onboard with the **TerminAttr** agent and a **token**, through the management VRF, or on their own with
  **ZTP**.
- Configuration through **configlets** or **Studios**, applied by reviewed and approved **change controls**.
- Any change outside CloudVision creates drift: decide on a rule and stick to it.
