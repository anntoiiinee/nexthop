At the bottom of every chapter on this site, the "Official documentation" section cites **RFCs**: RFC 8200 for IPv6,
RFC 4271 for BGP, RFC 7348 for VXLAN. Vendors talk about them constantly too: "RFC 7432 compliant", "supports
RFC 8092". An RFC is one of the documents that define how the Internet works. Knowing what it is, and how to read one,
lets you settle a disagreement between two vendors, understand why a protocol behaves the way it does, or check a
claim found on a forum.

## Why standards

An Arista switch exchanges BGP with a Cisco router, an Android phone reaches a Linux web server, a home router talks
to a site hosted on another continent. None of this would work if each vendor invented its own way to build an IP
packet or a TCP session. You need **written, public and shared rules**: header formats, message order, the meaning of
each field, expected behavior on errors. That is **interoperability**, and it is what lets an enterprise network be
**multi-vendor**.

## Who writes networking standards

Several bodies share the work, each on its own layer:

| Body | What it standardizes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **IETF** (*Internet Engineering Task Force*) | Internet protocols, from layer 3 to applications | IP, TCP, UDP, BGP, OSPF, DNS, TLS, HTTP, VXLAN, EVPN, published as **RFCs** |
| **IEEE** | layers 1 and 2 of local networks | Ethernet (802.3), VLANs (802.1Q), LACP (802.1AX), LLDP (802.1AB), Wi-Fi (802.11) |
| **ITU-T** and **ISO** | telecommunications and reference models | the OSI model (X.200, ISO/IEC 7498-1), telephone networks |
| **IANA** | **registries** of unique values | protocol numbers, TCP and UDP ports, AS numbers, address blocks |
| **RIRs** (RIPE NCC in Europe, ARIN, APNIC…) | regional allocation of IP addresses and AS numbers | your IPv6 /48, your public AS number |

This tutorial focuses on IETF RFCs, the most numerous and most cited in networking.

## Where the name comes from

**RFC** stands for *Request for Comments*. The name dates back to 1969: young researchers building ARPANET, the
Internet's ancestor, wanted to share their technical notes without seeming to impose anything. Steve Crocker published
**RFC 1**, "Host Software", on April 7, 1969, as a simple invitation to discuss. The name stuck, even though RFCs now
describe standards used by billions of devices. There are now more than 9,000 of them.

## How a draft becomes an RFC

The IETF is an open community: there is no membership, anyone can join the mailing lists and meetings. The work

1. An idea takes the form of an **Internet-Draft**, public, numbered by version. A draft expires after six months if it
   isn't updated: it is never a standard.
2. A **working group** (BGP has its own, so does IPv6) discusses, amends and produces successive versions.
3. When the group reaches **consensus**, the document goes through review by the whole IETF, then by the **IESG**,
   which validates its technical quality.
4. The **RFC Editor** publishes it with a final **number**.

The IETF's motto sums up the spirit: *"rough consensus and running code"*, broad agreement rather than a vote, and code
that works rather than theory. Vendors often implement a protocol **while it is still a draft**: EVPN over VXLAN was
deployed at scale for years before becoming RFC 8365.

## RFC categories

Not every RFC is a standard. Each RFC carries a **category** in its header:

| Category | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Proposed Standard** | a standard, stable and implementable | most protocols you use, such as VXLAN-EVPN (RFC 8365) |
| **Internet Standard** | a mature, widely deployed standard; it also gets an **STD** number | TCP (STD 7), IPv6 (STD 86) |
| **Best Current Practice** (BCP) | an operational best practice | BCP 38 (ingress filtering of spoofed addresses), BCP 14 (the meaning of MUST and SHOULD) |
| **Informational** | information, a description, with no standard status | RFC 7938 on BGP in data centers |
| **Experimental** | an experiment | protocols being tested |
| **Historic** | an outdated RFC, no longer to be used | old protocol versions |

<Callout type="note">
"Proposed Standard" is not a second-rate standard: it is the level of the vast majority of protocols in production.
The intermediate "Draft Standard" level was even removed in 2011 (RFC 6410): only two steps remain on the standards
track.
</Callout>

## An RFC never changes

This is the rule that surprises most: a published RFC is **never modified**. To fix or evolve a protocol, a **new** RFC
is published, declaring its relationship with the old one in its header:

- **Obsoletes**: the new RFC **replaces** the old one, which should no longer be read as the reference. TCP was
  described by RFC 793 since 1981; RFC 9293, in 2022, obsoletes it. IPv6 went from RFC 2460 to RFC 8200 in 2017.
- **Updates**: the new RFC **adds to** the old one, which remains valid. You then need to read both.
- **Errata** list errors reported after publication, on each RFC's page.

The benefit is huge: "RFC 4271" always refers to exactly the same text, whether you read it today or in twenty years.
The drawback: always check that an RFC hasn't been replaced or updated before relying on it.

### MUST, SHOULD, MAY

Normative RFCs use uppercase keywords, with the precise meaning defined by **RFC 2119** and **RFC 8174** (grouped as
BCP 14):

| Keyword | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **MUST**, REQUIRED, SHALL | mandatory: an implementation that doesn't do it isn't compliant |
| **MUST NOT**, SHALL NOT | forbidden |
| **SHOULD**, RECOMMENDED | recommended: you may deviate, but only for a good reason, knowing the consequences |
| **SHOULD NOT**, NOT RECOMMENDED | discouraged, with the same nuance |
| **MAY**, OPTIONAL | optional: an implementation may or may not do it |

RFC 9293 goes as far as numbering each of its requirements. For example, about the TCP checksum:

> The TCP checksum is never optional. The sender MUST generate it (MUST-2) and the receiver MUST check it (MUST-3).

<Callout type="tip">
These keywords only have this meaning in **uppercase**, and only when the RFC declares that it uses them (it says so at
the start, citing BCP 14). Some RFCs, such as RFC 8200 on IPv6, spell out their requirements in plain words, without
these keywords: read the sentences carefully then.
</Callout>

### Structure

An RFC almost always follows the same outline: an abstract, the status, an introduction with terminology, the
technical core (often with **ASCII header diagrams**, bit by bit), security considerations (mandatory), IANA
considerations (new values to register), then references. To understand a protocol, read the introduction and the
diagrams first; to settle a detail, search for the keyword in the text.

## Where to find RFCs

- **rfc-editor.org**: the official publication site, with each RFC as text, HTML and PDF, its information page
  (category, RFCs that replace or update it) and its errata.
- **datatracker.ietf.org**: tracking of drafts and working groups, to see what is in progress and where a document
  stands.
- The RFC Editor's search engine finds an RFC by number, title or keyword.

## Standard and implementation

A standard says what a protocol **must** do. It doesn't guarantee that two vendors behave **the same**:

- **SHOULD** and **MAY** items leave choices, and default values differ (timers, enabled options);
- vendors add proprietary **extensions**, or implement a **draft** before the final RFC;
- a datasheet saying "RFC 7432 compliant" doesn't always state which parts are supported.

When two devices don't get along, the RFC serves as the **referee**: it says which one is outside the standard. It is
often the first argument in a ticket opened with a vendor.

## April Fools' RFCs

The IETF has a sense of humor too. Every April 1st, a few parody RFCs come out, written with the utmost apparent
seriousness:

- **RFC 1149** (1990) describes the transmission of IP datagrams by **carrier pigeons**; it was even actually tested, in
  Norway, in 2001;
- **RFC 2324** (1998) defines HTCPCP, the **coffee pot** control protocol, and its **418 "I'm a teapot"** error code,
  which some web servers still return today.

They are classified as *Informational*: real RFCs, but not standards.

## Key takeaways

- An **RFC** is a document published by the **IETF**; some are standards, others best practices or information: the
  **category** is in the header.
- The path: **Internet-Draft**, working group, **consensus**, IESG, publication with a final number.
- An RFC **never changes**: a new RFC **obsoletes** or **updates** it. Always check both mentions.
- **MUST** is mandatory, **SHOULD** recommended, **MAY** optional (BCP 14), in uppercase only.
- The **IEEE** standardizes Ethernet and Wi-Fi, the **IANA** keeps the registries, the **RIRs** allocate addresses.
- The standard says what is expected; implementations differ. The RFC is the referee.
