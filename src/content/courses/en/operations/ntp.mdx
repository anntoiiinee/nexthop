<Term id="ntp" /> (*Network Time Protocol*) gives every device the **same time**, to within a few milliseconds. It
is one of the most discreet services on the network, and one that all the others depend on.

## Why time is critical

| What depends on time | What happens if it is wrong |
|---|---|
| **Logs** (syslog, firewall, TACACS+) | impossible to reconstruct an incident across several devices |
| **Certificates** (TLS, 802.1X, IPsec VPN) | a certificate not yet valid or already expired: connections refused |
| **Kerberos** (Active Directory) | beyond a 5-minute skew by default, authentication fails |
| **One-time codes** (MFA, TOTP) | codes rejected, users locked out |
| **Monitoring and telemetry** | shifted graphs, false correlations |
| **Databases, distributed storage** | write conflicts, failing replication |

Some sectors even have regulatory accuracy requirements: finance (timestamping of stock orders), telecoms
(synchronization of 4G and 5G antennas), broadcasting.

## How NTP measures time

An NTP client queries a server over <Term id="udp" /> on port **123**. The exchange holds **four timestamps**:

/>

The client derives:

- the network round-trip **delay**: `(T4 − T1) − (T3 − T2)`, the total time minus the time spent in the server;
- its **offset** from the server: `((T2 − T1) + (T3 − T4)) / 2`.

This calculation assumes the trip out and the trip back take as long. That's NTP's limit: on a local network,
accuracy is around a millisecond, or better; across the Internet, from a few milliseconds to a few tens. An
**asymmetric** path skews the result by half the difference.

The client doesn't trust a single exchange. It queries regularly (every 64 to 1,024 seconds), queries **several
servers**, discards inconsistent ones, and corrects its clock **gently**: it speeds it up or slows it down slightly,
rather than making it jump, so that logs stay in order. Only a large offset, at startup, is corrected in one step.

| Stratum | What it is |
|---|---|
| **0** | the **reference clock** itself: GNSS receiver, <Term id="atomic-clock">atomic clock</Term>, radio signal. It isn't on the network |
| **1** | a server **directly connected** to a stratum 0 clock |
| **2** | a server synchronized to a stratum 1 server |
| **3 to 15** | each level synchronizes to the previous one |
| **16** | **unsynchronized**: the device has no valid source |

The stratum measures a **distance**, not a **quality**: a stratum 2 server on your local network often gives better
time than a stratum 1 server on the other side of the Internet, because the path's delay and asymmetry matter more
than the rank.

## A local time reference: the GNSS appliance

To depend on nobody, a company installs its own **stratum 1** servers: **time appliances** (Meinberg, Microchip,
Oscilloquartz…), connected to a **GNSS antenna** on the roof.

- **The antenna** receives signals from satellite constellations (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou). Each satellite
  carries <Term id="atomic-clock">atomic clocks</Term>, and its signal carries the time: that's what the appliance picks up. The antenna needs a
  **clear view of the sky**, a cable of limited length, and lightning protection.
- **The internal oscillator** keeps time when the signal disappears (failed antenna, jamming): that's **holdover**.
  An oven-controlled crystal oscillator (OCXO) drifts by a few microseconds to a few milliseconds per day depending
  on its quality. A **rubidium atomic oscillator**, an option on high-end appliances, only drifts by around a
  microsecond per day.
- **Outputs**: NTP for everyone, and often **PTP** (*Precision Time Protocol*, IEEE 1588) for sub-microsecond needs
  (telecoms, finance, broadcasting). PTP requires switches that support it.

Install **at least two**, ideally on two sites, with separate antennas.

<Callout type="warning">
The GNSS signal is very weak, and therefore easy to **jam** or, worse, to **spoof**: a nearby transmitter broadcasts
a fake signal and shifts the time of all your devices. Choose appliances that detect these attacks, compare them
with at least one independent source (another site, external servers), and monitor their GNSS reception status.
</Callout>

## An external time reference: public servers

The alternative, much simpler, is to synchronize to servers **on the Internet**:

| Source | Specifics |
|---|---|
| **pool.ntp.org** | thousands of volunteer servers, grouped by country (`fr.pool.ntp.org`); ideal for a workstation or a small site |
| **National laboratories** | stratum 1 servers backed by a country's reference atomic clocks |
| **Providers** | Cloudflare (`time.cloudflare.com`, with NTS), carriers, clouds for their machines |

The benefit: nothing to install. The limits:

- a **dependency on the Internet**: without access, no fresh reference (the clock holds, then drifts);
- **UDP 123** must be allowed outbound, at least for your internal NTP servers;
- classic NTP is **not authenticated**: a forged answer can shift the time. **NTS** (*Network Time Security*,
  RFC 8915) adds authentication through TLS; Cloudflare, Netnod and several national laboratories offer it.

<Callout type="note">
Some providers (Google, Amazon) **smear** leap seconds: instead of adding one second at once, they slow their clock
down over several hours (*leap smear*). Never mix, on the same client, servers that smear and servers that don't:
during the smear, they disagree by up to half a second.
</Callout>

- **A few internal NTP servers** (two to four) synchronize to the references: GNSS appliances, external sources, or
  both. They are the only ones going out to the Internet.
- **Every other device** synchronizes to these internal servers, never directly to the Internet.
- Each client has **three or four sources**, not two: with two disagreeing sources, it can't tell which one is
  wrong; with three or more, the majority wins.
- **Everyone on UTC**, in logs as in device clocks: local time is only for display to humans. Daylight saving
  changes disappear from the logs.
- In an **Active Directory** domain, the controller holding the PDC emulator role synchronizes to the internal NTP
  servers; workstations follow the domain hierarchy.

## Configuration

### Arista EOS

```text title="Arista EOS"
ntp server vrf MGMT 192.0.2.123 prefer iburst key 10
ntp server vrf MGMT 192.0.2.124 iburst key 10
ntp server vrf MGMT 192.0.2.125 iburst key 10
ntp local-interface vrf MGMT Management1
ntp authentication-key 10 sha1 <shared-key>
ntp trusted-key 10
ntp authenticate
clock timezone Europe/Paris
```

- `vrf MGMT`: servers are reached through the [management network](/en/courses/operations/oob). On EOS, all NTP
  servers must be in the **same VRF**.
- `iburst`: sends a burst of requests at startup, to synchronize in a few seconds rather than a few minutes.
- `prefer`: all else being equal, this server is chosen first.
- `key` and `ntp authenticate`: answers must be signed with the shared key, also configured on the server. EOS
  accepts `md5` and `sha1`, and the documentation states that the key is stored **in clear text** in the
  configuration: protect backups accordingly.
- `clock timezone` changes the **display**; the clock and NTP exchanges stay in UTC.

### Cisco IOS

```text title="Cisco IOS"

clock summer-time CEST recurring last Sun Mar 2:00 last Sun Oct 3:00
```

Checking: `show ntp status` and `show ntp associations`, with the same columns as on EOS.

### Linux: chrony

On servers, `chrony` has largely replaced the old `ntpd` daemon: it converges faster, handles virtual machines and
intermittent connections better, and supports NTS.

```text title="/etc/chrony/chrony.conf of an internal NTP server"
server ntp-gnss1.corp.example iburst prefer   # local GNSS appliances
server ntp-gnss2.corp.example iburst
server time.cloudflare.com iburst nts        # authenticated external source
server ptbtime1.ptb.de iburst
allow 10.0.0.0/8                             # serves time to internal devices
makestep 1.0 3                               # step allowed only on the first 3 measurements
```

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| The device stays at stratum 16, "unsynchronised" | UDP 123 filtered, server in the wrong VRF, or a different authentication key |
| One hour off on some devices | wrong time zone, or daylight saving handled by hand |
| Logs from the same incident out of order | devices synchronized to different sources, or not at all |
| A virtual machine's time keeps jumping | both the hypervisor and NTP correct the clock: keep only one |
| Two sources, a time that oscillates | two disagreeing servers: add a third |
| The NTP server is used for amplification attacks | an old `ntpd` exposed with monitoring queries (`monlist`): restrict allowed queries and filter access |
| Everything shifts on the same day, everywhere | a wrong reference source (spoofed GNSS, single server) propagated to all: diversify references |

## Key takeaways

- Logs, certificates, Kerberos and MFA depend on time: the whole network must be **synchronized**, on **UTC**.
- NTP (UDP 123) measures **delay** and **offset** with four timestamps, queries **several servers** and corrects the
  clock gently. Accuracy: a millisecond locally, a few tens across the Internet.
- **Strata** count the steps from the reference clock: **0** for the reference (GNSS, atomic), **1** for the server
  connected to it, **16** for "unsynchronized". They measure a distance, not a quality.
- **Local**: GNSS appliances with an antenna and an oscillator (crystal or rubidium) for autonomy and accuracy, but
  beware of **jamming** and **spoofing**. **External**: simple, but dependent on the Internet; prefer **NTS**.
- Architecture: a few **internal servers**, **three or four sources** per client, authentication, and never a mix of
  servers that smear leap seconds and servers that don't.
