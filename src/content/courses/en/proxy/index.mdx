The firewall decides **who may talk to whom**, based on addresses and ports. It does not see much more:
a connection to port 443 may be webmail, a software update or malware exfiltrating data. This hop is
about the devices that work **at the application level**: they terminate the connection, read the
<Term id="http" /> request and decide what to do with it.

They exist in both directions:

- **outbound**, the forward <Term id="proxy">proxy</Term> controls users' browsing: who goes where, with which
  account, and what they downloaded;
- **inbound**, the reverse proxy and the load balancer publish the company's applications: a single entry
  point, several servers behind it, and a <Term id="waf" /> to filter attacks.

In the middle sit <Term id="tls" /> and certificates: almost all traffic is encrypted, and none of this
works without understanding them.

## What you will learn

| Chapter | What you will be able to do |
|---|---|
| **Forward proxy** | Choose between explicit and transparent proxy, distribute the configuration (PAC, WPAD), authenticate, filter and log |
| **TLS, certificates and inspection** | Read a certificate chain, build an internal authority, understand and frame TLS decryption |
| **Reverse proxy** | Publish an internal application, terminate TLS, route by host name or path, pass on the client address |
| **Load balancing and F5 BIG-IP** | Pick an algorithm, write health checks, handle persistence and SNAT, speak the F5 vocabulary |
| **WAF and application protection** | Understand the OWASP Top 10, tune a WAF without breaking the application, rate-limit |

The chapters build on each other: the reverse proxy reuses the certificates from the TLS chapter, and
load balancing starts from the reverse proxy.

## The lab

One lab serves every chapter. A workstation and a Squid proxy in the LAN, an HAProxy in front of two web
servers in the DMZ, and an "Internet" with three public sites, all connected by an Arista cEOS switch.
