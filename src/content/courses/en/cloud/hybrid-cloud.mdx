A company almost never moves entirely to the cloud: the directory, some databases, telephony or factories
stay in the data center, and cloud applications need to reach them. **Hybrid cloud** is that link between the
two worlds. Two building blocks from earlier hops come back, [IPsec VPN](/en/courses/security/ipsec-vpn) and
[BGP](/en/courses/routing/bgp-basics), used exactly as between two sites.

## Two ways to connect

| | Site-to-site VPN | Direct Connect |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | the Internet, encrypted with IPsec | a dedicated physical link, in an AWS point of presence |
| Provisioning | a few minutes | a few weeks (order, cross-connect, carrier) |
| Throughput | up to 1.25 Gbit/s per tunnel | from 50 Mbit/s (through a partner) to 100 Gbit/s and more |
| Latency and jitter | the Internet's, variable | stable and predictable |
| Encryption | yes, it is IPsec | **no** by default (MACsec on some links, or a VPN on top) |
| Cost | low: hourly per connection, plus traffic | hourly port fee, plus traffic (cheaper per GB), plus the carrier |

The most common design combines both: **Direct Connect for traffic, a VPN as backup**. Teams often start
with the VPN alone, which can be up in a day.

## The site-to-site VPN

An AWS **VPN connection** links two ends:

- on the AWS side, a **Virtual Private Gateway** (attached to a single VPC) or, better, the previous
  chapter's **Transit Gateway**, which gives access to every VPC;
- on the data center side, the **Customer Gateway**, an object describing your router: its public IP
  address and its AS number.

Each connection includes **two IPsec tunnels**, ending on two distinct AWS devices, in two different zones.
AWS performs maintenance on its devices one tunnel at a time: **bring up both tunnels**, otherwise every
maintenance cuts the link.

### Static or BGP?

A VPN connection can use **static** routing (the data center's prefixes are declared by hand) or **dynamic**
routing, with a **BGP** session in each tunnel. Choose BGP: it detects a tunnel failure and moves traffic,
and new data center networks are announced without touching AWS.

Each tunnel gets a small interconnection network in `169.254.x.x/30`: the first usable address is on the AWS
side, the second on the data center side. AWS provides, in the console, a ready-to-use configuration file for
many devices (Cisco, Juniper, Fortinet, Palo Alto, strongSwan…) with every IPsec and BGP parameter. The BGP
part looks like this, here in Arista EOS syntax:

```text title="Data center VPN router: BGP to AWS (example addresses)"
router bgp 65001
   router-id 10.10.255.1
   neighbor AWS peer group
   neighbor AWS remote-as 64512
   neighbor AWS timers 10 30
   neighbor 169.254.21.1 peer group AWS
   neighbor 169.254.22.1 peer group AWS
   network 10.10.0.0/16
```

The `10 30` timers (keepalive 10 s, hold 30 s) are the ones AWS recommends: losing a tunnel is detected
within 30 seconds at most. To go faster, AWS also supports DPD on the IPsec side.

<Callout type="warning">
An IPsec tunnel's MTU is smaller than 1500 bytes: the ESP header and the new IP header take up room. Without
adjustment, small requests go through and large transfers hang, the classic symptom seen in the
[TCP and UDP](/en/courses/fundamentals/tcp-udp) chapter. AWS recommends clamping the TCP MSS to 1379 bytes on
tunnel interfaces (`ip tcp adjust-mss 1379` or equivalent).
</Callout>

### In the lab

The lab's `vpn.tf` file creates the full connection (Virtual Private Gateway, Customer Gateway, VPN
connection, BGP route propagation into the private table) when `enable_vpn` is `true`. After deployment,
`terraform output vpn_tunnels` gives the public addresses of both AWS endpoints and each tunnel's
`169.254.x.x` addresses, and `terraform output -json vpn_preshared_keys` gives the pre-shared keys.

## Direct Connect

**Direct Connect** is a physical link between your network and AWS, established in a **Direct Connect point
of presence** (a colocation data center where AWS has equipment; there are several in and around Paris). Two
ways to get one:

- a **dedicated connection**: a 1, 10, 100 or 400 Gbit/s port, cross-connected between your device and
  AWS's in the point of presence;
- a **hosted connection**: a partner (carrier) that already has links to AWS allocates you capacity, from
  50 Mbit/s to several Gbit/s. It is the usual route when you have no equipment in the point of presence.

On that link, you create **virtual interfaces** (VIFs), each in its own 802.1Q VLAN, with its own BGP
session:

| Virtual interface | Gives access to |
|---|---|
| **Private** | one VPC (through a Virtual Private Gateway) or several, through a Direct Connect Gateway |
| **Transit** | one or more Transit Gateways, through a Direct Connect Gateway |
| **Public** | AWS public services (S3, for example) at their public addresses, without going through the Internet |

The **Direct Connect Gateway** is a global object: it lets one interface reach VPCs or Transit Gateways in
several regions.

<Callout type="field">
A single Direct Connect link is a single point of failure: a cable pulled in the point of presence, a carrier
maintenance, and the data center loses the cloud. AWS recommends at least **two connections in two different
points of presence** for critical workloads. Failing that, a backup VPN over the Internet is the economical
option, provided you **actually test the failover**: a backup that has never been tried doesn't work the day
you need it.
</Callout>

## Controlling the path

As soon as there are two paths (two tunnels, or Direct Connect plus a VPN), you must decide which one is
used, and in which direction. The tools from the [BGP attributes](/en/courses/routing/bgp-attributes) chapter
come back.

### From the data center to AWS

Your router decides: **LOCAL_PREF** is the natural tool. Raise it on routes learned over Direct Connect, or
over the preferred tunnel, and it applies to every router in the AS.

### From AWS to the data center

AWS decides, according to a documented priority order. For the same prefix, from most to least preferred:

| Order | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 1 | the **most specific** prefix (as everywhere in routing) |
| 2 | routes learned over **Direct Connect** |
| 3 | **static** routes of a VPN connection |
| 4 | routes learned over **BGP** through a VPN |
| 5 | between several BGP VPNs: the shortest **AS_PATH**, then the lowest **MED** |

Practical consequences:

- with Direct Connect and a backup VPN announcing the same prefix, **Direct Connect wins** with no extra
  work;
- to make a tunnel or VPN connection secondary, **lengthen the AS_PATH** of its announcements (*AS-path
  prepending*);
- beware of more specific prefixes: announcing a /24 over the VPN and the /16 over Direct Connect sends that
  /24 through the VPN, whatever the path priority;
- on Direct Connect, AWS accepts **BGP communities** that set your routes' LOCAL_PREF on its side
  (`7224:7100` low, `7224:7200` medium, `7224:7300` high), to choose between several connections.

```text title="Making a VPN secondary: lengthen the AS_PATH (Arista EOS)"
route-map RM-AWS-BACKUP-OUT permit 10
   set as-path prepend 65001 65001 65001
!
router bgp 65001
   neighbor 169.254.31.1 route-map RM-AWS-BACKUP-OUT out
```

<Callout type="tip">
An asymmetric path (out over Direct Connect, back over the VPN) works for routing, but breaks stateful
firewalls, which only see one direction of the connection. Tune **both directions** consistently, then check
with a `traceroute` from each side.
</Callout>

## Azure and Google Cloud equivalents

| AWS | Azure | Google Cloud |
|---|---|---|
| Site-to-Site VPN, Virtual Private Gateway | **VPN Gateway** (in a `GatewaySubnet` subnet of the VNet) | **Cloud VPN** (HA VPN: two interfaces, 99.99% SLA) |
| BGP sessions handled by the gateway | BGP on the VPN Gateway | **Cloud Router**, which carries the BGP sessions |
| Direct Connect | **ExpressRoute** | **Cloud Interconnect** (Dedicated or Partner) |
| Direct Connect Gateway | ExpressRoute Global Reach, Virtual WAN | Network Connectivity Center |

## Key takeaways

- The **site-to-site VPN** runs over the Internet, is up in minutes, encrypts everything, and often serves as
  backup.
- Each AWS VPN connection has **two tunnels**, to two distinct endpoints: **bring up both**, with **BGP**, so
  failover is automatic.
- **Direct Connect** is a dedicated physical link with stable throughput and latency, **unencrypted** by
  default; its **virtual interfaces** (private, transit, public) each carry a BGP session.
- On the data center side, **LOCAL_PREF** picks the outbound path; on the AWS side, the **most specific
  prefix**, then Direct Connect before VPN, then **AS_PATH** and MED.
- Tune both directions consistently: an asymmetric path breaks stateful firewalls.
