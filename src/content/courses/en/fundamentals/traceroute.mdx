"The site is slow", "the application doesn't respond": before looking for a culprit, you want to know **which way
the packets go** and **where they stop**. `traceroute` (`tracert` on Windows) lists the routers crossed, hop by
hop. It is one of the most used tools… and one of the most misread. This chapter explains how it works, then why
it is so often misleading, or unusable.

## The principle: the TTL and ICMP messages

Every IP packet carries a **TTL** field (*Time To Live*, called *Hop Limit* in IPv6). Each router that forwards the
packet decreases it by 1. If it reaches 0, the router drops the packet and sends the sender an <Term id="icmp" />
**Time Exceeded** message (type 11), with its own address as the source. The TTL exists so that a packet caught in
a routing loop eventually disappears.

Traceroute hijacks this mechanism:

1. it sends a packet with a **TTL of 1**: the first router drops it and answers "Time Exceeded". Hop 1 is known;
2. then a packet with a **TTL of 2**: it gets past the first router, dies on the second, which reveals itself;
3. and so on, until the **destination** itself answers, or the maximum is reached (30 hops by default).

How does traceroute know it has arrived? It depends on the probe type:

| Variant | Probe sent | Destination's answer | Command |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UDP** | UDP to an unlikely port (33434 and up) | ICMP **Port Unreachable**: no service listens on that port | `traceroute` on Linux, Cisco and Arista |
| **ICMP** | ICMP **Echo Request**, like a ping | ICMP **Echo Reply** | `tracert` on Windows, `traceroute -I` on Linux |
| **TCP** | TCP **SYN** to a real port (443, 22…) | **SYN-ACK** if the port is open, **RST** if closed | `traceroute -T -p 443`, `tcptraceroute` |

In every case, intermediate routers answer the same way: **Time Exceeded**. By default, traceroute sends **three
probes** per hop, hence the three times shown.

```text title="client:~# traceroute -n 10.10.2.10"
traceroute to 10.10.2.10 (10.10.2.10), 30 hops max, 60 byte packets
 1  10.10.1.254  1.665 ms  1.499 ms  1.463 ms
 2  10.10.12.2  1.404 ms  1.258 ms  1.182 ms
 3  10.10.2.10  1.114 ms  0.968 ms  0.885 ms
```

- Each line is a **hop**, with the address that answered and the round-trip time of each of the three probes.
- The address shown is usually the router's **ingress interface**, the one the probe arrived on: R1 appears as
  `10.10.1.254`, R2 as `10.10.12.2`. Not its loopback address, nor its egress one.
- `-n` skips the reverse lookup of each address. Without it, traceroute shows names (the
  [PTR records](/en/courses/fundamentals/dns#ptr-from-an-address-to-a-name)), often very telling on the Internet,
  but each lookup slows down the trace.
- A star `*` means: **no answer within the timeout** (5 seconds by default). Not necessarily packet loss, as we'll
  see.

## Why traceroute lies, or doesn't work

Traceroute relies on error messages that nobody is required to send, or to let through. It only sees devices that
**decrement the TTL and agree to answer**, and only on the **forward** path. Here are the cases you will meet.

### 1. A router doesn't answer, but forwards just fine

R2 is configured not to send "Time Exceeded" messages, a very common practice to be less visible:

```text title="R2 no longer sends Time Exceeded"
traceroute to 10.10.2.10 (10.10.2.10), 30 hops max, 60 byte packets
 1  10.10.1.254  0.109 ms  0.021 ms  0.018 ms
 2  * * *
 3  10.10.2.10  0.095 ms  0.076 ms  0.069 ms
```

Hop 2 is silent, but hop 3 answers: **traffic gets through**. Stars in the middle of a trace that continues are
**never** a problem in themselves.

### 2. The destination doesn't answer the probes

This time, the server filters incoming UDP, like many servers and firewalls:

```text title="The server filters UDP (traceroute -n -m 6)"
traceroute to 10.10.2.10 (10.10.2.10), 6 hops max, 60 byte packets
 1  10.10.1.254  0.847 ms  0.789 ms  0.766 ms
 2  10.10.12.2  0.745 ms  0.672 ms  0.639 ms
 3  * * *
 4  * * *
 5  * * *
 6  * * *
```

It looks like the path stops after R2. In reality, the server does receive the probes: it just doesn't answer.
Change the probe type, and the trace completes:

```text title="Same network, ICMP probes (traceroute -n -I) or TCP probes (traceroute -n -T -p 443)"
traceroute to 10.10.2.10 (10.10.2.10), 30 hops max, 60 byte packets
 1  10.10.1.254  0.076 ms  0.023 ms  0.013 ms
 2  10.10.12.2  0.032 ms  0.018 ms  0.019 ms
 3  10.10.2.10  0.033 ms  0.023 ms  0.048 ms
```

<Callout type="field">
To test an application's path, run a **TCP trace to the application's port** (`traceroute -T -p 443`, or
`tcptraceroute`): it crosses the same firewalls and follows the same rules as the real traffic. A UDP or ICMP trace
may be blocked where the application gets through, or the other way round.
</Callout>

### 3. ICMP answers are rate-limited

A router forwards packets with its hardware chips, but **builds** ICMP messages with its CPU, slowly and at low
priority. To protect itself, it **limits their number** (on Cisco and Arista, that's the job of *Control Plane
Policing*, CoPP). Linux does it too by default. `mtr`, which repeats traceroute continuously, shows it well:

The reading rule: **only loss that carries on to the destination counts**. Loss, or high latency, on an
intermediate hop that disappears at the following hops comes from the router **answering** slowly, not from a
**forwarding** problem.

### 4. The return path is invisible

Traceroute shows the **forward** path. Answers come back along the path the routers choose, which can be
completely different: that's **asymmetric routing**, very common on the Internet and between sites. The times shown
are round trips, so they include this unknown return path. A "slow" hop may be slow because of the return. To see
the return, you need a trace run **from the other end**, or the carrier's *looking glass*.

### 5. Several paths at once (ECMP)

When several equal-cost paths exist (<Term id="ecmp" />), routers spread flows by hashing addresses and **ports**.
But UDP traceroute changes the destination port with every probe: each probe may take a different path. As a
result, a single line mixes several routers, and the trace draws a path that doesn't exist:

```text title="Trace across two ECMP paths (illustration, made-up addresses)"
 3  198.51.100.1  4.1 ms  198.51.100.5  4.3 ms  198.51.100.1  4.0 ms
 4  198.51.100.9  5.2 ms  5.1 ms  198.51.100.13  5.4 ms
```

**Paris traceroute** and `mtr` in TCP or UDP mode with a fixed port keep the same ports for every probe, and
therefore the same path.

### 6. Hidden hops: MPLS, tunnels and overlays

- **MPLS**: carriers may not copy the TTL into the MPLS label. The whole core network then appears as **a single
  hop**, or disappears. Others show it, with the labels in the answer.
- **Tunnels** (IPsec, GRE, VXLAN, SD-WAN): the original packet is encapsulated, and its TTL only drops at both ends
  of the tunnel. Ten routers between two sites become **a single hop**.
- **Cloud networks**: providers' virtual networks don't show their internal devices.

### 7. Devices that don't count

Anything that doesn't **route** is invisible: layer 2 switches, but also firewalls in transparent mode, and some
devices that forward without decrementing the TTL. Conversely, a **proxy** or **load balancer** that terminates TCP
connections answers a TCP trace itself: it stops on it, not on the server behind.

### 8. Misleading addresses

The router answers with the address of its choice: the ingress interface, sometimes another, sometimes a **private**
address (`10.x`, `172.16.x`) in the middle of an Internet path, impossible to reach or identify. Reverse names are
written by carriers, sometimes outdated. Cross-check before concluding.

## What traceroute really says

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| `* * *` in the middle, then the trace continues | a router that doesn't answer: **no problem** |
| `* * *` all the way, from one hop onwards | traffic stops there… **or** just stops answering. Change the probe (`-I`, `-T`) |
| High latency on one hop, normal afterwards | slow ICMP answer from that router: **ignore** |
| Latency that rises at one hop and **stays** high afterwards | the delay is real: a long link (ocean, satellite) or congestion at that point |
| Loss on one hop, 0% at the destination | ICMP rate limiting: **ignore** |
| Loss starting at one hop and **continuing** to the destination | real loss, around that hop |
| `!H`, `!N`, `!X` after a time | the router answers "host unreachable", "network unreachable" or "prohibited" (filtering) |
| The same addresses repeating in a loop | a **routing loop** |

<Callout type="warning">
A trace is not proof. Before blaming a carrier or a team, confirm with what the devices themselves say: the routing
table (`show ip route`), interface error and drop counters, a packet capture, or the firewall's flow logs.
</Callout>

## On network devices

```text title="Arista EOS"
traceroute 10.10.2.10
traceroute vrf MGMT 192.0.2.53
```

```text title="Cisco IOS"
traceroute 10.10.2.10
traceroute 10.10.2.10 source Loopback0
```

The **source** matters: a trace run from a router uses its egress interface address by default, which isn't
necessarily advertised in the network. Answers then can't find their way back, and the trace only shows stars. Use
a reachable source, such as the loopback.

For traceroute to work through your firewalls, they must let back the ICMP **Time Exceeded** and **Destination
Unreachable** messages related to the probes. Modern firewalls do so by tying them to the original session.

## mtr: continuous traceroute

`mtr` (*My Traceroute*) combines ping and traceroute: it sends probes in a loop and shows, for each hop, the loss
rate and latency statistics. It is the ideal tool for an **intermittent** problem: `mtr -n -r -c 100 destination`
produces a report after 100 probes, to attach to a ticket with a carrier. `mtr -T -P 443` uses TCP probes. The
reading rules are the same: only loss that carries on to the destination counts.

## Key takeaways

- Traceroute sends probes with an **increasing TTL**; each router where the TTL reaches 0 answers **ICMP Time
  Exceeded**, the destination answers **Port Unreachable** (UDP), **Echo Reply** (ICMP) or **SYN-ACK / RST** (TCP).
- Linux, Cisco and Arista use **UDP** by default, Windows **ICMP**; `-T -p <port>` follows an application's real
  path.
- It only sees devices that **decrement the TTL and answer**, and only the **forward** path.
- **Stars**, **loss** or **latency** on an intermediate hop that don't carry on to the destination are not problems:
  filtering or ICMP rate limiting.
- **ECMP**, **MPLS**, **tunnels**, **cloud** and **proxies** distort or hide the path. Always cross-check with
  routing tables and counters.
