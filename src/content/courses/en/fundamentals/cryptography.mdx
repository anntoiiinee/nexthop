A packet crossing a network goes through many hands: switches, routers, carriers, public Wi-Fi access points. Any
of these intermediaries can read it, copy it or change it. Encryption is what makes that journey safe, and you meet
it everywhere in the job: HTTPS, IPsec VPNs, SSH to devices, SNMPv3, WPA Wi-Fi, and certificates that always expire
at the worst moment. This chapter explains the four mechanisms all of this relies on, with the two characters every
cryptography course has used since the 1970s: **Alice** and **Bob**.

## Alice, Bob and Eve

Alice wants to send a message to Bob. Between them, a network they don't control, and **Eve** (the *eavesdropper*),
who listens to everything passing by. Sometimes even **Mallory** (*malicious*), who doesn't just listen: she changes
messages, or impersonates one of them.

Alice and Bob need three guarantees:

| Guarantee | The question | The mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Can Eve read the message? | encryption |
| **Integrity** | Did Mallory change the message on the way? | the fingerprint (hashing) |
| **Authenticity** | Does the message really come from Alice? | the signature, then the certificate |

Encryption alone isn't enough: an encrypted message can be altered, or sent by someone else. Real protocols combine
all three.

## Symmetric encryption: a single key

It is the oldest and most intuitive form: **the same key** encrypts and decrypts, like a combination padlock whose
code both Alice and Bob know.

The reference algorithm is <Term id="aes" />, with a 128- or 256-bit key. It is **extremely fast**, often
accelerated by the processor itself: it encrypts the data of an HTTPS session, an IPsec tunnel or an SSH
connection, at several gigabits per second.

Its problem isn't technical, it is logistical: **how do Alice and Bob exchange key K?** If they send it over the
network, Eve reads it on the way. If they must meet to share it, encryption only works between people who already
know each other. And with a hundred correspondents, you need a hundred different keys.

<Callout type="note">
The **pre-shared key** (PSK) of an IPsec VPN or a WPA2-Personal Wi-Fi network is exactly that: a secret the
administrator typed in on both sides. It works for two sites, becomes painful for fifty, and every leak means
changing the key everywhere.
</Callout>

## Asymmetric encryption: a key pair

In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published an idea that changed everything: what if the key that
encrypts were **not** the one that decrypts? Everyone then owns a **key pair**, mathematically linked:

- a **private key**, which its owner keeps secret and never sends;
- a **public key**, which they hand out to everyone, without any risk.

What one key does, only the other can undo. The most telling analogy: Bob hands out **open padlocks** (his public
key) to anyone, and keeps the only **key** that opens them (his private key).

The key exchange problem disappears: the public key can travel in clear text, Eve can copy it, it is useless to
her. Common algorithms are <Term id="rsa" /> and **elliptic curve** ones (ECDSA, Ed25519, X25519), which offer the
same security with much shorter keys.

The downside: asymmetric encryption is **hundreds to thousands of times slower** than symmetric, and only encrypts
small messages. You never encrypt a data stream with RSA.

## The fingerprint: checking integrity

A **hash function** turns any data, one byte or one terabyte, into a fixed-size **fingerprint**: 256 bits for
SHA-256. It has three properties:

- the same data **always** gives the same fingerprint;
- the slightest change gives a **completely different** fingerprint;
- it is practically impossible to recover the data from the fingerprint, or to craft two pieces of data with the
  same fingerprint.

That is how you check that a downloaded EOS image hasn't been corrupted or tampered with: compare its SHA-256
fingerprint with the one Arista publishes. A fingerprint alone doesn't protect against Mallory, though: if she
changes the message, she recomputes the fingerprint. It must be tied to a key, which is what a signature does.

<Callout type="warning">
A hash is **not** encryption: it can't be "decrypted", and it hides nothing in a reversible way. MD5 and SHA-1 are
broken today (collisions can be crafted) and must no longer be used for security. Use SHA-256 or stronger.
</Callout>

## The signature: proving who wrote it

A signature uses asymmetric keys **the other way round**. Alice computes her message's fingerprint and transforms
it with **her private key**: that is the signature. Bob verifies with **Alice's public key**.

Mallory can't forge a valid signature without Alice's private key, and if she changes the message, the old
signature no longer matches. A signature thus provides **integrity** and **authenticity**.

| | Encrypting for Bob | Signing as Alice |
|---|---|---|
| You use | the recipient's (Bob's) **public** key | the sender's (Alice's) **private** key |
| Decrypted or verified with | Bob's **private** key | Alice's **public** key |
| What you get | confidentiality | integrity and authenticity |

That is the table to remember: it prevents the most common confusion about key pairs.

## Diffie-Hellman: agreeing on a secret in public

One problem remains: symmetric is fast but the key must be shared; asymmetric solves sharing but is slow. The
**Diffie-Hellman exchange** lets Alice and Bob **build a common secret key together**, exchanging only public
values. Eve sees everything go by, and can't deduce the key.

The classic analogy uses paint: mixing two colors is easy, separating a mix is impossible.

In reality, colors are numbers, and mixing is a mathematical operation that is easy one way and impossible to
reverse. The modern variant, ECDHE (on elliptic curves), is used by TLS 1.3, IPsec (IKEv2) and SSH. The final **E**
means *ephemeral*: a new key is built for each session, then thrown away. If the server's private key is stolen one
day, recorded past sessions stay unreadable: that is **forward secrecy**.

## In practice: everything is hybrid

No protocol uses a single technique. They combine them, each for what it does best:

| Step | Technique | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Agree on a session key | ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (ECDHE) | no key travels, and forward secrecy |
| 2. Prove the server's identity | signature (RSA, ECDSA, Ed25519) + certificate | stop Mallory from impersonating it |
| 3. Encrypt the data | symmetric (AES-GCM, ChaCha20) | speed |
| 4. Check every packet | authentication code (built into AES-GCM) | detect any change on the way |

That is exactly the TLS 1.3 handshake, detailed in the
[TLS, certificates and inspection](/en/courses/proxy/tls-pki) chapter. IPsec (with IKEv2) and SSH follow the same
pattern.

## The weak link: whose public key is this?

Everything relies on one assumption: when Alice encrypts with "Bob's public key", it **really** is Bob's. If Mallory
manages to make her believe her own public key is Bob's, everything collapses: that is the **man-in-the-middle
attack**.

Encryption works perfectly, and yet Mallory reads everything. The countermeasure: **bind a public key to an
identity** in a verifiable way. Two approaches exist:

- **the certificate**: a trusted third party, the certificate authority (<Term id="ca" />), signs a document saying
  "this public key belongs to `www.example.com`". The browser checks that signature with the authority's public key,
  already present in the system. That is the <Term id="pki" />, used by HTTPS and often by IPsec;
- **the fingerprint checked by hand**: that is what SSH does. On the first connection, it shows the device key's
  fingerprint and asks you to confirm it; then it remembers it in `known_hosts` and warns if it changes.

<Callout type="field">
The famous SSH warning `REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED` means exactly: "this device's public key is no
longer the one I remembered". Most often, the device was replaced or reinstalled, and its new key is legitimate.
But it is also precisely what you would see with a man in the middle. Before removing the old entry with
`ssh-keygen -R <address>`, check that the change is expected.
</Callout>

## Where you meet them in networking

| Use | What is encrypted or signed | The keys |
|---|---|---|
| **HTTPS, TLS** | web traffic, APIs (eAPI, NetBox) | server certificate, ECDHE, AES |
| **SSH** | device administration | the device's host key, and your personal key instead of a password |
| **IPsec VPN** | traffic between two sites | pre-shared key or certificates, IKEv2 and Diffie-Hellman, then AES (see [IPsec VPN](/en/courses/security/ipsec-vpn)) |
| **SNMPv3** | monitoring | one authentication key and one encryption key per user |
| **WPA2 / WPA3 Wi-Fi** | the radio link | shared key (Personal) or 802.1X with certificates (Enterprise) |
| **MACsec** | an Ethernet link, between two devices | AES keys managed by MKA |
| **Software images** | a downloaded file's integrity | SHA-256 fingerprint, sometimes the vendor's signature |

### Key sizes

| Use | Recommended today | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetric | AES-128 or AES-256 (GCM mode) | DES, 3DES, RC4 |
| Asymmetric RSA | 2048 bits minimum, 3072 to last | 1024 bits |
| Elliptic curves | P-256, Ed25519, X25519 | |
| Hashing | SHA-256, SHA-384 | MD5, SHA-1 |
| Diffie-Hellman | groups 14 (2048 bits) and above, or ECDH (groups 19, 20, 31) | groups 1, 2 and 5 |

<Callout type="note">
A sufficiently powerful quantum computer would break RSA, elliptic curves and Diffie-Hellman. It doesn't exist yet,
but an attacker can record exchanges today to decrypt them tomorrow. New so-called **post-quantum** algorithms
(such as ML-KEM, standardized in 2024) are therefore starting to be deployed, first in browsers and TLS, alongside
current algorithms. Symmetric encryption, for its part, stays solid with 256-bit keys.
</Callout>

## In the lab: Alice and Bob with OpenSSL

All you need is a Linux terminal (or WSL) with OpenSSL, installed almost everywhere. The outputs below were obtained
with OpenSSL 3.0; the keys generated on your machine will differ, which is expected.

**1. Bob creates his key pair** (RSA), and extracts the public key to hand out:

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm RSA -pkeyopt rsa_keygen_bits:3072 -out bob.key
openssl pkey -in bob.key -pubout -out bob.pub
```

**2. Alice encrypts a message for Bob**, with Bob's public key only:

```bash
printf 'Rendez-vous au datacenter a 14h' > message.txt
openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -pubin -inkey bob.pub -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in message.txt -out message.enc
wc -c message.txt message.enc
```

```text title="Result"
 31 message.txt
384 message.enc
```

The 31-byte message becomes a 384-byte block (the RSA key size, 3072 bits) that looks like noise. Encrypt it twice:
the result changes every time, thanks to OAEP's random padding.

**3. Bob decrypts** with his private key, and gets `Rendez-vous au datacenter a 14h` back:

```bash
openssl pkeyutl -decrypt -inkey bob.key -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -in message.enc
```

**4. Alice signs**, this time with an Ed25519 key (elliptic curve), and Bob verifies with Alice's public key:

```bash
openssl genpkey -algorithm ed25519 -out alice.key
openssl pkey -in alice.key -pubout -out alice.pub
openssl pkeyutl -sign -inkey alice.key -rawin -in message.txt -out message.sig
openssl pkeyutl -verify -pubin -inkey alice.pub -rawin -in message.txt -sigfile message.sig
```

```text title="Result"
Signature Verified Successfully
```

**5. Mallory changes the message** (14h becomes 15h) and keeps the signature:

```bash
printf 'Rendez-vous au datacenter a 15h' > fake.txt
openssl pkeyutl -verify -pubin -inkey alice.pub -rawin -in fake.txt -sigfile message.sig
```

```text title="Result"
Signature Verification Failure
```

**6. Your own SSH key**, to log into devices without a password:

```bash
ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "first@laptop"
ssh-keygen -lf ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub
```

```text title="Result (your fingerprint will differ)"
256 SHA256:egVafpgIwgiWwJZs83f8qXnbu4nq8q/bjEx5D27S+lY first@laptop (ED25519)
```

The `id_ed25519.pub` file is the public key: copy it to devices (on Arista EOS, with `username <name> ssh-key
<key>`). The `id_ed25519` file, with no extension, is the private key: it never leaves your computer.

## Key takeaways

- Three guarantees: **confidentiality** (encryption), **integrity** (fingerprint), **authenticity** (signature,
  certificate).
- **Symmetric**: a single key, very fast (AES), but it must be shared.
- **Asymmetric**: a **public key** you hand out and a **private key** you keep; slow, used for small messages,
  signatures and key exchanges.
- **Encrypting for Bob**: with Bob's public key. **Signing as Alice**: with Alice's private key.
- **Diffie-Hellman** builds a common key without sending it; in its ephemeral form, it provides forward secrecy.
- Protocols are **hybrid**: Diffie-Hellman for the key, signature and certificate for identity, AES for the data.
- Without a verified link between a key and an identity, the **man in the middle** wins: that is the role of
  **certificates** and SSH fingerprints.
