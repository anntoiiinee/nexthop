A 48-port switch, cables everywhere, and a simple question: **what is plugged into port 23?** Rather
than tracing the cable by hand in the rack, you ask the switch. It knows, thanks to <Term id="lldp" />
or its Cisco equivalent, <Term id="cdp" />.

## The principle

Every device regularly sends, on each of its ports, a small frame that says **who it is**: its name,
the port it is sending from, its model, its management address… Its direct neighbor receives it and
stores this information in a **neighbor table**.

Two important properties:

- announcements are sent to a **special multicast address** that switches never forward: you only
  discover your **direct neighbors**, one hop away;
- they are **independent of IP**: they work even on a port without an address, in any VLAN. That is
  what makes them an excellent layer 2 troubleshooting tool.

## LLDP: the standard

**LLDP** (Link Layer Discovery Protocol) is defined by the **IEEE 802.1AB** standard. Every vendor
supports it: Arista, Cisco, Juniper, but also Linux servers (the `lldpd` daemon), hypervisors, IP
phones and Wi-Fi access points.

| Parameter | Default value |
|---|---|
| Destination address | `01:80:c2:00:00:0e` (multicast, never forwarded) |
| EtherType | `0x88cc` |
| Transmission | every **30 seconds** |
| Validity (TTL) | **120 seconds**: without a new announcement, the neighbor disappears from the table |

### A frame made of TLVs

The information in an LLDP frame is stored as **TLVs** (Type, Length, Value): each piece of
information has a type, a size and a content. The first three TLVs are mandatory.

An extension, **LLDP-MED** (Media Endpoint Discovery), adds TLVs for telephony: the switch tells the
IP phone which **voice VLAN** to use, and the phone announces its power draw (PoE).

## CDP: the Cisco version

**CDP** (Cisco Discovery Protocol) is the proprietary ancestor of LLDP, present on every Cisco device.
The principle is identical.

| | LLDP | CDP |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | IEEE 802.1AB, multi-vendor | Cisco proprietary |
| Destination address | `01:80:c2:00:00:0e` | `01:00:0c:cc:cc:cc` |
| Interval / validity | 30 s / 120 s | 60 s / 180 s |
| Enabled by default | Arista EOS: yes · Cisco IOS: no | Cisco IOS: yes |
| Telephony | LLDP-MED | native (voice VLAN, PoE) |

So, **LLDP or CDP?** In a multi-vendor network, **LLDP**, since it is the only one everybody speaks.
In a 100% Cisco network, CDP works very well, but enabling LLDP as well costs nothing and prepares for
other equipment. Both can run at the same time.

<Callout type="note">
Arista EOS doesn't speak CDP natively: facing a Cisco switch, enable LLDP on the Cisco side
(`lldp run`) and the two will see each other.
</Callout>

## Configuration

```text title="Arista EOS"
lldp run                          ! enabled by default
lldp timer 30
lldp hold-time 120
!
interface Ethernet1
   description to-internet
   no lldp transmit               ! don't announce ourselves to the outside
   no lldp receive
```

```text title="Cisco IOS"
cdp run                           ! enabled by default
lldp run                          ! disabled by default: enable it
!
interface GigabitEthernet1/0/1
 description to-internet
 no cdp enable
 no lldp transmit
 no lldp receive
```

## What it's used for in production

- **Finding a device**: "server srv-01 is plugged into SW1, port Et20", without going down to the
  server room.
- **Checking the cabling**: compare the actual LLDP neighbors with the planned cabling. Automation
  tools do it at scale: a cable swapped between two ports stands out immediately.
- **Documenting automatically**: scripts or tools like NetBox rebuild the full topology from the LLDP
  tables of every switch.
- **Configuring IP phones**: LLDP-MED or CDP give them their voice VLAN without manual configuration.

<Callout type="field">
During a migration or a fabric deployment, validating LLDP neighbors is among the very first automated
checks: if every link connects the expected port to the expected port, a large share of "it won't come
up" problems disappear before you even look at routing.
</Callout>

## What about security?

LLDP and CDP broadcast useful information… including to an attacker: device name, exact model,
software version (and therefore its known vulnerabilities), management IP address. Hence a simple rule:
**disable LLDP and CDP on untrusted ports** (links to the Internet or to an ISP, ports in public areas),
and keep them on internal links, where they are extremely helpful.

## In the lab

The lab from the static routing chapter is a good fit: once deployed, type `show lldp neighbors` on
`r2`. You will see `r1` and `r3`, with each one's port, even though no route is configured yet: LLDP
doesn't depend on IP.

## Key takeaways

- LLDP and CDP announce a **device's identity to its direct neighbors**, one hop away.
- **LLDP** (IEEE 802.1AB) is the multi-vendor standard; **CDP** is the Cisco equivalent.
- An LLDP announcement is made of **TLVs**: Chassis ID, Port ID and TTL are mandatory.
- `show lldp neighbors` (or `show cdp neighbors`): what is plugged into which port.
- Disable them on **untrusted** ports: they reveal model and version.

We now move back up to layer 3 with **IPv4 addressing**.
