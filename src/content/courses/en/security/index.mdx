The previous hops built a network that **works**: packets find their way, links are redundant, the
configuration is automated. This hop is about making it **safe**: deciding who may talk to whom, linking
sites across the Internet without exposing traffic, and limiting the damage the day a machine is
compromised.

The central tool is the **firewall**. This hop explains what it really does (a session table, not just a
list of rules), how to write and maintain a policy that is still readable five years later, how to build an
<Term id="ipsec" /> VPN between two sites, and how to split the network so an attacker cannot go everywhere
once inside.

## What you will learn

| Chapter | What you will be able to do |
|---|---|
| **Firewall basics** | Tell an ACL from a stateful firewall, read a session table, configure zones and NAT, see what an NGFW adds |
| **Security policies** | Write a policy in the right order, spot overly broad or shadowed rules, organise the rule lifecycle |
| **IPsec VPN** | Understand IKEv2 and ESP, choose algorithms, build a route-based VPN, troubleshoot a tunnel that will not come up |
| **North-south and east-west traffic** | Tell flows crossing the perimeter from those flowing inside, place controls and avoid hairpinning |
| **Segmentation** | Split the network into zones, build a flow matrix, control access with 802.1X and NAC, apply zero trust |

Examples are given for three vendors, in equal measure: **Fortinet** (FortiGate), **Palo Alto Networks**
and **Stormshield**. The concepts are the same everywhere; only the vocabulary changes.

## The lab

A headquarters and a branch office, each behind its firewall, connected to the "Internet" by an Arista
cEOS router. Commercial firewalls do not run freely in ContainerLab: the lab firewalls are Linux, with
**nftables** for filtering and NAT, and **strongSwan** for the IPsec VPN. The mechanisms are exactly those
of a FortiGate or a Palo Alto: session table, zones, NAT, IKEv2.
