For a long time, enterprise network security boiled down to a **perimeter**: a strong firewall between the Internet
and the inside, and broad trust inside. That model looks at traffic **entering and leaving**. It forgets traffic
**flowing inside**, between computers, servers and applications, even though it is now the largest share, and the
path an attacker takes once in. Network and security engineers tell them apart with two compass directions:
**north-south** and **east-west**.

## Where the names come from

The picture comes from data center diagrams: the Internet and the WAN are drawn **at the top**, servers **at the
bottom**, and devices of the same tier **side by side**.

| | North-south | East-west |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Traffic **entering** or **leaving** the network | **Internal** traffic, between machines of the same network |
| **Examples** | an Internet client to the website, a computer to the Internet, a branch to the data center | the web server to the application, the application to the database, a computer to a file share, a backup |
| **Volume** | a minority of a data center's traffic | the majority: a single client request triggers many internal exchanges |
| **Historical control** | the perimeter firewall, the proxy, the WAF | often none, hence the problem |

<Callout type="note">
The boundary depends on what you look at. For a data center, a branch's traffic arriving over the WAN is
**north-south**: it crosses the data center's boundary. For the whole company, it is internal traffic. The terms
help place controls, not classify flows once and for all: always state which boundary you mean.
</Callout>

## Why east-west became the main topic

Three changes moved the problem:

- **Applications were split up.** A monolithic application on one server became a front end, services, databases,
  caches and message queues, each on its own machine or container. What was a function call inside a server became
  an east-west network flow.
- **Attacks go through the inside.** An attacker rarely gets in through a perimeter firewall flaw. They get in
  through a computer (an attachment, a stolen password), then **move laterally**, towards the directory, shares,
  backups. Each of these moves is an east-west flow.
- **The perimeter dissolved.** Remote work, SaaS applications, cloud: part of the users and servers are no longer
  behind the perimeter firewall. Trusting "the inside" no longer makes sense; that is the starting point of
  [zero trust](/en/courses/security/segmentation).

<Callout type="field">
In ransomware incident reports, the pattern almost always repeats: a modest initial access, then days, sometimes
weeks, of internal movement before encryption. The perimeter firewall saw nothing, because nothing crossed it.
Controlling and observing east-west traffic gives you a chance to detect and contain the attack during that phase.
</Callout>

## Controlling north-south

North-south is network security's historical ground, and its tools are well established. They all sit at the
**crossing point** between inside and outside:

| Direction | Control | Role | See |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inbound | perimeter firewall, <Term id="dmz" /> | only expose published services, isolated in the DMZ | [Firewall basics](/en/courses/security/ngfw-basics) |
| Inbound | reverse proxy, <Term id="waf" />, <Term id="ddos" /> protection | protect published web applications | [Reverse proxy](/en/courses/proxy/reverse-proxy), [WAF](/en/courses/proxy/waf) |
| Outbound | proxy, URL filtering, TLS inspection | control what computers and servers reach on the Internet | [Forward proxy](/en/courses/proxy/forward-proxy) |
| Between sites | IPsec VPN, SD-WAN | connect branches to headquarters without exposing traffic | [IPsec VPN](/en/courses/security/ipsec-vpn) |

North-south has one advantage: **everything goes through the same place**. A properly sized firewall, two in high
availability, see every flow, log it and filter it.

## Controlling east-west

East-west has no natural crossing point: two servers in the same VLAN talk directly, through the switch, without
ever crossing a firewall. You must therefore **create** these control points, at different levels of granularity.

| Level | Tool | What it controls | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Between large zones | **internal firewall**, gateway of the VLANs or <Term id="vrf">VRFs</Term> | users to servers, servers to backups, DMZ to inside | sees nothing inside a zone |
| Between subnets | **ACLs** on the core or the leaves | fast, stateless filtering, close to the source | quickly unreadable, no logs nor application awareness |
| Between machines of a zone | **private VLAN**, port isolation | computers only reach their gateway, not their neighbors | all or nothing |
| Per application, per machine | **micro-segmentation**: hypervisor distributed firewall, server agent, cloud security groups | every flow between two workloads | requires knowing each application's flows precisely |

You progress in that order. The internal firewall between large zones already stops most lateral movement, and
[segmentation](/en/courses/security/segmentation) details how to cut those zones.

### The price of a central firewall: hairpinning

Sending east-west traffic through a central firewall has a cost. Two neighboring servers, on the same switch, in two
different zones, talk by going all the way up to the firewall, then back down: that is **hairpinning**.

For occasional administration or backup traffic, that is acceptable. For application flows of several gigabits per
second, the firewall becomes the data center's bottleneck, and its capacity a major cost line. Three answers exist:

- **choose the boundaries**: only send flows **between zones of different sensitivity** through the firewall, and
  let flows inside a trusted zone go directly;
- **the firewall at the fabric edge**: in an [EVPN-VXLAN](/en/courses/datacenter/evpn) fabric, each zone is a VRF,
  and routing **between VRFs** only happens on *border leaves* (or *service leaves*) connected to the firewall.
  Routing **inside** a VRF stays distributed on each leaf, with no hairpinning;
- **distributed micro-segmentation**: control happens on the hypervisor or the server itself, where traffic starts.
  No detour, but one more tool to operate.

### Symmetry, again

A stateful firewall must see **both directions** of a connection. East-west, there are more paths than at the
perimeter: several firewalls, several gateways, ECMP routing. If the way out goes through one firewall and the way
back through another path, the firewall only sees half the dialogue and drops packets. It is the most frequent
cause of "ping works, the application doesn't" outages after inserting an internal firewall. Firewall clusters sync
their sessions for that reason, and in the cloud, the Transit Gateway's **appliance mode** guarantees the same thing
(see [Interconnecting VPCs](/en/courses/cloud/transit-gateway)).

## Seeing east-west

At the perimeter, firewall logs show everything. Inside, with no crossing point, traffic is invisible by default. To
make it visible:

- **internal firewall logs**, for what crosses them: including **denies**, which reveal a poorly documented
  application or an attempt to move around;
- **NetFlow, sFlow or IPFIX** exported by switches and leaves: who talks to whom, how much, on which ports, even
  without a firewall (see [SNMP, syslog and NetFlow](/en/courses/operations/monitoring));
- **cloud flow logs**, such as [VPC Flow Logs](/en/courses/cloud/security-groups);
- **network detection** tools (NDR), which learn usual behavior and flag a computer that starts scanning its
  neighbors' ports or reaching the directory like an administrator.

<Callout type="tip">
Before writing a single east-west rule, observe. A few weeks of NetFlow or logs from a firewall in "allow everything
and log" mode give the real flow matrix, the one nobody had documented. That is the approach described at the end
of the [Segmentation](/en/courses/security/segmentation) chapter.
</Callout>

## Encryption changes the game

North-south, outbound web traffic can be decrypted on the proxy ([TLS inspection](/en/courses/proxy/tls-pki)).
East-west, more and more flows are encrypted **end to end** between services, with certificates specific to each
service (the *mutual TLS* of service meshes such as Istio or Linkerd). An internal firewall then sees addresses and
ports, not the content. The consequence is a deep trend: east-west policies are increasingly written **by identity**
(this service, this security group, this service account) rather than by IP address, and enforced close to the
workloads.

## In the cloud

The same notions show up, under other names:

| | AWS | Azure | Google Cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inbound north-south** | Internet Gateway, load balancer, WAF | Application Gateway, Azure Firewall | Cloud Load Balancing, Cloud Armor |
| **Outbound north-south** | NAT Gateway, AWS Network Firewall | NAT Gateway, Azure Firewall | Cloud NAT |
| **East-west between VPCs** | Transit Gateway and inspection VPC | hub with Azure Firewall, or Virtual WAN | Network Connectivity Center, appliances |
| **East-west inside a VPC** | security groups (by group reference) | NSGs, Application Security Groups | firewall rules by tag or service account |

[Security groups](/en/courses/cloud/security-groups), attached to each interface and able to reference another group
rather than an address, are exactly east-west micro-segmentation, available by default.

## In the lab

This hop's `security` lab contains both kinds of flows. The headquarters firewall, `fw-hq`, separates four zones:
outside, users, servers and the DMZ, plus the IPsec tunnel to the branch.

<Lab name="security" lang="en" />

1. Open `fw-hq/nftables.nft` ([Firewall basics](/en/courses/security/ngfw-basics) chapter) and classify each rule of
   the `transit` chain: R10 to R12 (users to the Internet) and R30 (Internet to the DMZ) are **north-south**; R15, R20
   (users to servers) and R40 to R42 (branch and headquarters) are **east-west** from the company's point of view.
2. Generate traffic in both directions, then look at each rule's counters:

   ```bash
   docker exec clab-security-pc-hq curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' http://10.10.20.10
   docker exec clab-security-ext curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' http://203.0.113.2
   docker exec clab-security-fw-hq nft list chain inet filter transit
   ```

3. A lateral move: from the user computer, try to reach the server's fake database, on port 5432. It answers
   `base de donnees srv-hq` (the lab is in French):

   ```bash
   docker exec clab-security-pc-hq nc -w 2 10.10.20.10 5432
   ```

   Nothing justifies a computer reaching a database. Rule **R15**, an overly broad "temporary" east-west permission,
   allows it: exactly the path an attacker would take from a compromised computer. (If you already removed it in the
   [Security policies](/en/courses/security/security-policies) chapter, put it back for this exercise.)
4. Remove R15, reload the policy, and try again: the intranet (port 80) still answers, this time thanks to the
   precise rule R20; the database no longer answers, and the implicit deny counter, at the end of `transit`, goes up.
   The Security policies chapter showed R15 as a rule shadowing another one; seen from the east-west angle, it is
   above all an open door to lateral movement.

   ```bash
   docker exec clab-security-fw-hq nft -f /lab/nftables.nft
   ```

5. Question: in the lab, `pc-hq` is plugged directly into the firewall. If a second computer were added to the users
   network (`10.10.10.0/24`), connected to the first one through a switch, could `fw-hq` filter traffic between them?
   Why, and which tools could?

## Key takeaways

- **North-south**: what enters and leaves the network. **East-west**: what flows inside. Always state the boundary
  you mean (the site, the data center, the VPC).
- East-west is the **majority** of a data center's traffic, and the path of an attacker's **lateral movement**.
- North-south is controlled at the **crossing point**: perimeter firewall, DMZ, proxy, WAF, VPN.
- East-west has no natural crossing point: you create one, from the **internal firewall** between large zones down
  to **micro-segmentation** close to workloads.
- A central firewall causes **hairpinning**: keep it for boundaries between zones of different sensitivity, or route
  between VRFs on border leaves; and guarantee flow **symmetry**.
- **Observe** east-west traffic (logs, NetFlow, flow logs, NDR) before filtering it, and to detect what gets through.
