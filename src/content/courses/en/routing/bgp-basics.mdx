The Internet isn't one network, it is an **interconnection of tens of thousands of independent
networks**: ISPs, hosting providers, large companies, universities. Each one runs its network as it
sees fit, but they all need to exchange their routes. The protocol that makes this possible is
<Term id="bgp" /> (Border Gateway Protocol), and it is also at the heart of modern data centers.

## Autonomous systems

An **autonomous system** (<Term id="as" />) is a set of networks run by a single organization, with a
common routing policy. Each AS has a number (ASN):

| Range | Use |
|---|---|
| 1 to 64,495 | public 2-byte ASNs, assigned by the registries (RIPE in Europe) |
| **64,512 to 65,534** | **private** 2-byte: free to use internally, never advertised on the Internet |
| 4,200,000,000 to 4,294,967,294 | private 4-byte |

In our example, the company uses AS **65010**. It is connected to two ISPs, AS 65100 and 65200, which
are themselves connected to the rest of the Internet (here AS 65300).

The company wants two things: **advertise its prefix** `198.51.100.0/24` to the Internet (otherwise
nobody will know how to reply), and **learn the Internet's routes** to know which ISP to go out through.

## A BGP session

Unlike OSPF, BGP doesn't discover its neighbors: each neighbor (called a **peer**) is **configured
explicitly**, with its address and AS number. The two routers open a **TCP connection on port 179**,
and the whole exchange goes through it. TCP guarantees delivery: BGP doesn't need to repeat its
announcements, it only sends **changes**.

The session goes through several states:

<Callout type="field">
A session stuck in **Active** doesn't mean "active": TCP can't be established. The usual causes: wrong
neighbor address, no route to it, port 179 filtered, or an AS number different from what the other side
expects (the session then drops right after the OPEN, with an explicit error message in the logs).
</Callout>

Four message types travel through the session:

| Message | Role |
|---|---|
| **OPEN** | introduce yourself: AS number, router ID, timer, capabilities (IPv6, EVPN…) |
| **UPDATE** | advertise new routes with their attributes, or withdraw some |
| **KEEPALIVE** | "I'm still here", every **60 s**; with no news for **180 s** (hold time), the session drops |
| **NOTIFICATION** | report an error, right before closing the session |

## eBGP and iBGP

- **eBGP** (<Term id="ebgp" />): a session between two **different** ASes, here CE1 and each ISP. The
  peers are usually directly connected.
- **iBGP** (<Term id="ibgp" />): a session between two routers of the **same** AS, for example the
  company's two edge routers, to share what each one learned from its ISP.

The two types don't behave the same:

| | eBGP | iBGP |
|---|---|---|
| Administrative distance (Cisco) | 20 | 200 |
| AS_PATH | you prepend your own AS when advertising | unchanged |
| Next hop | replaced by your own address | **kept** (hence `next-hop-self`) |
| Re-advertisement | an eBGP route is advertised to every peer | a route learned over iBGP is **not** re-advertised to another iBGP peer |

The last rule prevents loops inside an AS, but requires every iBGP router to be connected to every other
one (full mesh) or to go through **route reflectors**. We'll come back to this in the data center course,
where it is the foundation of EVPN fabrics.

## Advertising a prefix

BGP **only advertises what you ask it to**. The cleanest method is the `network` statement, which
advertises a prefix **if it is present, exactly, in the routing table**:

- here, `198.51.100.0/24` is the connected network of CE1's LAN interface: it is in the table;
- if the advertised prefix is broader than the actual networks (a `/22` split into internal `/24`s), you
  add a static route to `Null0` so that it exists in the table.

```text title="Arista EOS: advertising an aggregate"
ip route 198.51.100.0/22 Null0
!
router bgp 65010
   network 198.51.100.0/22
```

<Callout type="warning">
On the Internet, you advertise **only prefixes that belong to you** (assigned by a registry or by your
ISP). Advertising someone else's prefix, even by mistake, can divert their traffic: this is a BGP
*hijack*, and they regularly make the news.
</Callout>

## AS_PATH and loop prevention

Every time a route goes from one AS to another over eBGP, the advertising AS **prepends its number** to
the **AS_PATH** attribute. When the company's prefix reaches the Internet, it therefore carries the full
list of ASes it went through:

This list serves two purposes:

1. **preventing loops**: a router that receives a route whose AS_PATH **already contains its own AS
   number** rejects it. A route can therefore never come back into the AS that originated it;
2. **choosing**: when nothing else decides, BGP prefers the path that crosses **the fewest ASes**. The
   next chapter covers all the criteria.

## Configuration

On CE1, declare both ISPs and advertise the company's prefix:

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS"
router bgp 65010
   router-id 10.255.0.10
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 remote-as 65100
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 description ISP-A
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 remote-as 65200
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 description ISP-B
   network 198.51.100.0/24
   !
   address-family ipv4
      neighbor 192.0.2.0 activate
      neighbor 192.0.2.2 activate
```

```text title="CE1 · Cisco IOS"
router bgp 65010
 bgp router-id 10.255.0.10
 neighbor 192.0.2.0 remote-as 65100
 neighbor 192.0.2.0 description ISP-A
 neighbor 192.0.2.2 remote-as 65200
 neighbor 192.0.2.2 description ISP-B
 !
 address-family ipv4
  network 198.51.100.0 mask 255.255.255.0
  neighbor 192.0.2.0 activate
  neighbor 192.0.2.2 activate
```

<Callout type="warning">
This minimal configuration **works but isn't safe**: CE1 accepts everything the ISPs send, and
re-advertises to each one what it learns from the other. Chapter 5 adds the filters you need before
going to production.
</Callout>

## Verifying

```text title="CE1#show ip bgp summary"
BGP summary information for VRF default
Router identifier 10.255.0.10, local AS number 65010
  Description  Neighbor    V AS     MsgRcvd MsgSent InQ OutQ  Up/Down State   PfxRcd PfxAcc
  ISP-A        192.0.2.0   4 65100       12      14   0    0 00:05:12 Estab   1      1
  ISP-B        192.0.2.2   4 65200       11      13   0    0 00:04:58 Estab   1      1
```

Both sessions are **Established**, and each ISP sends one prefix: the route to the Internet. The
company's prefix doesn't come back: its AS_PATH would contain AS 65010.

```text title="CE1#show ip bgp"
          Network                Next Hop         Metric  LocPref Weight  Path
 * >      198.51.100.0/24        -                -       -       0       i
 * >      203.0.113.0/24         192.0.2.0        0       100     0       65100 65300 i
 *        203.0.113.0/24         192.0.2.2        0       100     0       65200 65300 i
```

- `*`: the route is **valid** (its next hop is reachable); `>`: it is the **best** one, installed in the
  routing table.
- The Internet (`203.0.113.0/24`) is known through both ISPs, with AS_PATHs of the same length. BGP
  picked ISP-A thanks to a tie-breaker; the next chapter explains which one, and how to choose yourself.
- The trailing `i` is the **ORIGIN** attribute: the prefix was injected with `network`.

## The lab

The ISPs and the "Internet" are already configured. Only **CE1** is left to do:

1. Deploy the lab. On `host1`, `ping 203.0.113.1` fails.
2. On CE1, configure both eBGP sessions **without** the `network` statement. Check that they reach
   Established and that you receive `203.0.113.0/24`.
3. The ping still fails: the request goes out, but the Internet doesn't know `198.51.100.0/24`, so the
   reply has no way back. Add `network 198.51.100.0/24`: the ping works.
4. On `inet`, look at `show ip bgp 198.51.100.0/24`: which AS_PATHs do you see?
5. Try advertising a prefix that isn't yours (`network 10.99.0.0/16` with a route to Null0). Do the ISPs
   accept it? Why? Look at their configuration.

<Callout type="tip" title="Solution">
The CE1 configuration from the "Configuration" section is enough. In step 5, the ISPs only accept
`198.51.100.0/24` from CE1 (a prefix-list filter on their inbound session): that is exactly what real
ISPs do with their customers.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- An **AS** is a network with a single policy; ASNs **64,512 to 65,534** are private.
- BGP exchanges routes over a **TCP 179** session between **explicitly configured** peers; only an
  **Established** session exchanges routes.
- **eBGP** between different ASes, **iBGP** within an AS (next hop kept, no iBGP→iBGP re-advertisement).
- You only advertise what you ask for with `network`, provided the prefix is **in the routing table**.
- The **AS_PATH** lists the ASes crossed: it prevents loops and serves as a selection criterion.
