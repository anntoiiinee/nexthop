A BGP session with no filters is a door wide open in both directions: you can **advertise anything** to
the whole world, and **accept anything** from your neighbors. Almost all of the biggest Internet outages
come from there. A **routing policy** decides, for each neighbor, what you accept, what you advertise and
with which attributes.

## The trap: becoming a transit

Back to the minimal configuration from chapter 3. CE1 receives ISP-A's routes… and, since BGP advertises
its best routes to all its eBGP peers by default, it **re-advertises them to ISP-B**. Result: the company
presents itself as a possible path between its two ISPs.

If ISP-B picks this path (because it prefers its customers' routes, which is the rule among ISPs), part of
the Internet's traffic goes **through the company's link**, which saturates. This kind of **route leak**
has already taken down major services for hours.

<Callout type="warning">
Golden rule for a company: **advertise only your own prefixes to your ISPs**. Everything else must be
filtered outbound, on every session, without exception.
</Callout>

## The filtering tools

Three tools, which combine:

| Tool | What it matches | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Prefix-list** | the prefix and its length | "exactly 198.51.100.0/24", "anything inside 10.0.0.0/8" |
| **AS-path list** | the AS_PATH, with a regular expression | `^$`: routes originated in my AS · `^65100_`: received directly from ISP-A |
| **Route-map** | the conductor: `match` clauses (prefix-list, AS-path, community…) and `set` actions | "if the prefix is in PL-X, set LOCAL_PREF to 200" |

### Prefix-lists

A prefix-list matches the prefix **and its length**. The `ge` (greater or equal) and `le` (less or
equal) keywords widen the match:

| Entry | Matches |
|---|---|
| `permit 198.51.100.0/24` | exactly 198.51.100.0/24, not a /25 inside it |
| `permit 10.0.0.0/8 le 32` | 10.0.0.0/8 and every subnet inside it, down to /32s |
| `permit 0.0.0.0/0` | only the default route |
| `permit 0.0.0.0/0 le 24` | every route from /0 to /24 (more specific ones are refused) |

### Route-maps

A route-map is a series of **numbered clauses**, evaluated in order. Each clause is a `permit` or a
`deny` with conditions (`match`) and optionally modifications (`set`):

<Callout type="tip">
The **implicit deny** at the end of a route-map is the classic trap: a route-map containing a single
`permit` clause with a `match` silently rejects everything else. To "accept everything while modifying
some routes", end with a `permit` clause without `match`.
</Callout>

## CE1's policy

### Outbound: only our prefixes

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS"
ip prefix-list PL-OUR-PREFIXES seq 10 permit 198.51.100.0/24
!
route-map RM-ISP-A-OUT permit 10
   match ip address prefix-list PL-OUR-PREFIXES
!
route-map RM-ISP-B-OUT permit 10
   match ip address prefix-list PL-OUR-PREFIXES
   set as-path prepend 65010 65010
```

Thanks to the implicit deny, anything that isn't `198.51.100.0/24` is refused outbound: no more transit.
Belt and braces: you can also filter on the AS_PATH, with `^$`, which only matches routes **originated in
our AS** (empty AS_PATH before sending).

```text title="Arista EOS: AS-path filter"
ip as-path access-list AS-LOCAL permit ^$ any
```

### Inbound: nothing suspicious, and not too much

Inbound, refuse what no ISP should ever send us: **our own prefixes** (coming back), **private** and
reserved ranges (*bogons*), and overly specific prefixes.

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS"
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 10 permit 10.0.0.0/8 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 20 permit 172.16.0.0/12 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 30 permit 192.168.0.0/16 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 40 permit 100.64.0.0/10 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 50 permit 127.0.0.0/8 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 60 permit 198.51.100.0/24 le 32
ip prefix-list PL-BOGONS seq 70 permit 0.0.0.0/0 ge 25
!
route-map RM-ISP-A-IN deny 10
   match ip address prefix-list PL-BOGONS
route-map RM-ISP-A-IN permit 20
   set local-preference 200
!
route-map RM-ISP-B-IN deny 10
   match ip address prefix-list PL-BOGONS
route-map RM-ISP-B-IN permit 20
```

The `0.0.0.0/0 ge 25` line catches every prefix longer than a /24: by convention, the Internet doesn't
accept more specific routes. The `198.51.100.0/24 le 32` line refuses our own prefix.

### Capping the number of routes

If an ISP suddenly starts sending us hundreds of thousands of routes (a mistake on their side), our
router can be overwhelmed. Set a **ceiling** per session:

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS: the full session"
router bgp 65010
   router-id 10.255.0.10
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 remote-as 65100
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 description ISP-A
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 route-map RM-ISP-A-IN in
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 route-map RM-ISP-A-OUT out
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 maximum-routes 1000
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 remote-as 65200
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 description ISP-B
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 route-map RM-ISP-B-IN in
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 route-map RM-ISP-B-OUT out
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 maximum-routes 1000
   network 198.51.100.0/24
   !
   address-family ipv4
      neighbor 192.0.2.0 activate
      neighbor 192.0.2.2 activate
```

<Callout type="note">
On Cisco, the equivalent of `maximum-routes` is `neighbor … maximum-prefix`. With the full Internet
table (over a million IPv4 and IPv6 routes today), the ceiling is of course set accordingly; many
companies simply receive a **default route** from each ISP, which is enough to pick a primary and a
backup exit.
</Callout>

## Communities

A **community** is a 32-bit tag attached to a route, written `ASN:value`, for example `65010:100`. By
itself it changes nothing in routing: it is used to **convey an intention** that routers, at home or at
the neighbor's, turn into an action.

| Community | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `no-export` (65535:65281) | don't advertise this route outside the AS that receives it |
| `no-advertise` (65535:65282) | don't advertise it to any other peer |
| `blackhole` (65535:666, RFC 7999) | ask the ISP to drop traffic to this prefix (DDoS defense) |
| An ISP's communities | defined and published by each ISP, for example "65100:80 = set my LOCAL_PREF to 80" |

The last use is very powerful: instead of tinkering with prepending, you directly ask the ISP to lower
your route's preference **on their side**, by tagging your announcements with the community they
document.

```text title="Arista EOS: tagging our announcements to ISP-B"
route-map RM-ISP-B-OUT permit 10
   match ip address prefix-list PL-OUR-PREFIXES
   set community 65200:80
!
router bgp 65010
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 send-community
```

<Callout type="warning">
Communities are **not sent by default** on Arista EOS or Cisco IOS: without `send-community` on the
session, your tags never reach the neighbor.
</Callout>

## Applying a change

BGP only sends changes: modifying a route-map doesn't always immediately re-evaluate routes already
received. Depending on the platform, you trigger a **soft reset**, without tearing down the session:

```text
clear ip bgp 192.0.2.0 soft in     ! re-evaluate routes received from this neighbor
clear ip bgp 192.0.2.0 soft out    ! resend our announcements with the new policy
```

And always check what you actually receive and send:

```text
show ip bgp neighbors 192.0.2.0 received-routes      ! before the inbound policy (see below)
show ip bgp neighbors 192.0.2.0 routes               ! after the inbound policy
show ip bgp neighbors 192.0.2.0 advertised-routes    ! what we actually advertise
```

To see routes **before** filtering, the router has to keep them: that is the
`neighbor … rib-in pre-policy retain` option on Arista EOS, and `neighbor … soft-reconfiguration inbound`
on Cisco IOS. Without it, `received-routes` may stay empty.

## RPKI: checking who may advertise what

Filters protect us from our own mistakes, but how do you know whether a received prefix is advertised by
its **real owner**? That is the role of **RPKI** (<Term id="rpki" />): each holder publishes a
cryptographically signed **ROA** that says "this prefix may be advertised by this AS". Routers compare
every received route against these ROAs and reject invalid announcements.

<Callout type="field">
RPKI validation and strict filtering are part of the **MANRS** best practices (Mutually Agreed Norms for
Routing Security), adopted by most large ISPs. For a company, the bare minimum is to create the ROAs of
its own prefixes with its registry: it's free and protects it against hijacks.
</Callout>

## The lab

Still the `bgp-basic` lab, with the configuration from the previous chapter (no filters):

1. On `isp-b`, `show ip bgp`: do you see `203.0.113.0/24` **learned from CE1** (AS_PATH
   `65010 65100 65300`)? That's the leak. (Here ISP-B refuses it thanks to its own customer filter: look
   at `show ip bgp neighbors 192.0.2.3 received-routes` on ISP-B to see it arrive.)
2. Apply CE1's outbound policy and check with `advertised-routes` that only `198.51.100.0/24` is
   advertised now.
3. Apply the inbound policy with LOCAL_PREF on ISP-A. Compare `received-routes` with `routes`.
4. Add the `65200:80` community towards ISP-B, first without then with `send-community`, and look at
   what ISP-B receives (`show ip bgp 198.51.100.0/24` on isp-b).

## Key takeaways

- A company advertises **only its own prefixes** to its ISPs: otherwise, it becomes a **transit**.
- **Prefix-list** (prefixes), **AS-path list** (paths), **route-map** (match + set, ordered clauses,
  **implicit deny** at the end).
- Inbound: refuse **bogons**, your own prefixes, prefixes longer than /24, and **cap** the number of
  routes.
- **Communities** convey intentions; remember `send-community`.
- **RPKI** checks that an AS is allowed to advertise a prefix: create the ROAs of your prefixes.
