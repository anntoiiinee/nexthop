In the previous chapter, CE1 received the route to the Internet from **both** ISPs and picked one.
Based on what? And above all, how do you **impose** your choice, for example sending all traffic
through ISP A, which is faster, and keeping B as a backup? That is the whole art of BGP: each route
carries **attributes**, and you act on them.

## A route's attributes

A BGP route isn't just a prefix and a next hop: it is a prefix accompanied by a set of **attributes**
describing its origin, its path and everyone's preferences.

| Attribute | Category | Role | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NEXT_HOP** | mandatory | address to send the traffic to | — |
| **AS_PATH** | mandatory | list of ASes crossed | passed everywhere |
| **ORIGIN** | mandatory | how the route was born: `i` (network), `?` (redistribution) | passed everywhere |
| **LOCAL_PREF** | discretionary | **outbound** preference within my AS, higher = better | **within my AS only** (iBGP) |
| **MED** (<Term id="med" />) | optional | **inbound** preference suggested to a neighbor AS, lower = better | towards the neighbor AS |
| **COMMUNITY** | optional | tags used to apply policies | depends on configuration |
| Weight | Cisco-specific | preference local to **a single router** | never passed on |

## How BGP chooses

When several paths lead to the same prefix, BGP compares them **criterion by criterion, in a fixed
order**. As soon as one criterion decides, the comparison stops.

If a tie remains after these steps: depending on the vendor, the **oldest** path, then the neighbor's
**lowest router ID** wins. That is what happened in the previous chapter: both AS_PATHs were 2 ASes
long, everything was equal, and ISP-A won the final tie-breaker.

<Callout type="tip">
A way to remember the first steps, which are the ones you actually work with: **local preference
before short paths**. LOCAL_PREF first, shortest AS_PATH next.
</Callout>

## Choosing the way out: LOCAL_PREF

**Outbound** traffic (from the company to the Internet) is decided by CE1. To send everything through
ISP-A, raise the **LOCAL_PREF** of the routes received from ISP-A (100 by default):

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS"
route-map RM-ISP-A-IN permit 10
   set local-preference 200
!
router bgp 65010
   neighbor 192.0.2.0 route-map RM-ISP-A-IN in
```

LOCAL_PREF is the go-to tool for outbound traffic, because it is compared **before** the AS_PATH: it
wins even if ISP-B offers a shorter path. And it is shared over iBGP with every router in the AS: with
two edge routers, they all prefer the same exit.

```text title="CE1#show ip bgp 203.0.113.0/24"
BGP routing table entry for 203.0.113.0/24
 Paths: 2 available
  65100 65300
    192.0.2.0 from 192.0.2.0 (10.100.255.1)
      Origin IGP, metric 0, localpref 200, valid, external, best
  65200 65300
    192.0.2.2 from 192.0.2.2 (10.200.255.1)
      Origin IGP, metric 0, localpref 100, valid, external
      Not best: Local preference
```

The last line says it clearly: the path through ISP-B lost **because of LOCAL_PREF**.

## Choosing the way in: AS-path prepending

**Inbound** traffic (from the Internet to the company) is trickier: it is **the other ASes** that choose
how to reach us. We can only **influence** them. The most common tool: artificially lengthen the AS_PATH
of the announcements sent to the backup ISP, by repeating your own number.

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS"
route-map RM-ISP-B-OUT permit 10
   set as-path prepend 65010 65010
!
router bgp 65010
   neighbor 192.0.2.2 route-map RM-ISP-B-OUT out
```

<Callout type="note">
Prepending is a **suggestion**, not an order: an AS that set a higher LOCAL_PREF on the route learned
through ISP-B (because it is its customer, for example) will still use it. Beyond 3 or 4 repetitions, the
effect stops increasing and you just pollute the Internet's table.
</Callout>

## MED: when you have several links to the same neighbor

**MED** tells **one neighbor AS**, connected through several links, which one to use to reach us: the
lowest MED wins. By default, it is only compared between routes coming **from the same neighbor AS**. It
is therefore useless between ISP-A and ISP-B, but helpful with two links to the same ISP (a 10 Gb/s
primary, a 1 Gb/s backup):

```text title="CE1 · Arista EOS: backup link to the same ISP"
route-map RM-ISP-A-BACKUP-OUT permit 10
   set metric 200
```

## next-hop-self in iBGP

In iBGP, the next hop of a route learned over eBGP is **kept**. With two edge routers, CE2 receives from
CE1 a route whose next hop is ISP-A's address… which it can't reach. The route is then invalid (no `*`
asterisk), and ignored.

## Key takeaways

- Every BGP route carries **attributes**: NEXT_HOP, AS_PATH, ORIGIN, LOCAL_PREF, MED, communities…
- BGP compares paths **in a fixed order**: reachable next hop, **LOCAL_PREF** (highest), **AS_PATH**
  (shortest), ORIGIN, **MED** (lowest), eBGP before iBGP, IGP cost.
- **Outbound**: you decide with **LOCAL_PREF**, shared across the whole AS.
- **Inbound**: you influence with **AS-path prepending** (and MED towards a single neighbor).
- In iBGP, remember **next-hop-self**.

Last chapter: securing all of this with **routing policies**, so you never advertise what you shouldn't,
nor accept just anything.
