A fire in a room, a flood, a long power outage, a human error that wipes a storage array, ransomware that
encrypts every server: a data center **can** disappear, all at once. The question isn't whether it will happen, but
what the company will have prepared that day. That's the purpose of the **BCP** and the **DRP**. They are first of
all management and business topics, but the network plays a central role: without it, no recovery site can be
reached.

## BCP and DRP: two complementary plans

| | **BCP**, business continuity plan | **DRP**, disaster recovery plan |
|---|---|---|
| In French | *PCA, plan de continuité d'activité* | *PRA, plan de reprise d'activité* |
| Goal | **keep** the business running during the crisis, with little or no interruption | **resume** the business after it stopped, within a defined time |
| Scope | the whole company: premises, staff, suppliers, communication… and IT | usually IT: systems, data, network |
| In IT | **active/active** sites, a failure absorbed with no visible switchover | a **failover** to a recovery site, with an accepted interruption |

The BCP includes the DRP: the company's continuity also depends on IT's ability to restart.

- <Term id="rto" /> (*Recovery Time Objective*): the maximum acceptable downtime. "The ordering service must be back
  within 4 hours."
- <Term id="rpo" /> (*Recovery Point Objective*): the maximum acceptable data loss, expressed in time. "We accept
  losing at most 15 minutes of orders."

These numbers aren't technical: the **business** sets them, during a **Business Impact Analysis** (BIA): what does
an hour of downtime cost for each service, in money, reputation, legal obligations? A payroll service can wait two
days; an online shop, not two hours. Each application thus gets its RTO and RPO, and therefore its strategy, with
its cost.

## Recovery strategies

| Strategy | Principle | RTO | RPO | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Offsite backups** | backups kept elsewhere; everything is rebuilt on hardware to be found | days to weeks | last backup (often 24 h) | low |
| **Cold site** | premises and network ready, no running servers | days | last backup | moderate |
| **Warm site** | hardware installed, data restored or replicated regularly | hours | hours | high |
| **Hot site** (active/passive) | a complete site, data **replicated** continuously, ready to take over | minutes to hours | seconds to minutes | very high |
| **Active/active** | both sites serve all the time | close to zero | close to zero | highest |

The **cloud** changed the game: a recovery site can be infrastructure started only in case of disaster, paid as you
go.

## Data: replication and backups

### Replication

- **Synchronous**: a write is only confirmed once written **at both sites**. Zero RPO, but each write pays the round
  trip between the sites, which limits the distance (the [DCI](/en/courses/datacenter/dci) chapter does the math).
- **Asynchronous**: the write is confirmed locally, then sent to the other site. No distance limit, but an RPO of a
  few seconds to a few minutes.

### Why replication isn't enough

Replication copies **everything**, including the worst: a table deleted by mistake, or files encrypted by
ransomware, are replicated to the recovery site within seconds. Against those disasters, only **backups** protect
you, provided the attacker can't reach them.

The classic rule, known as **3-2-1**, popularized by photographer Peter Krogh and taken up by the US cybersecurity
agency (CISA):

- **3** copies of the data (the original and two backups);
- on **2** different media;
- **1** of them offsite.

Today we add an **offline or immutable** copy (impossible to change or delete during its retention period, even by an
administrator), and regular, error-free **restore tests**.

<Callout type="warning">
Ransomware looks for the backups first, to prevent recovery. Backups reachable from the production network, with the
same admin accounts as the servers, will be encrypted along with them. Isolate the backup network and accounts, and
keep a copy that no network access can erase.
</Callout>

## What the network must prepare

On failover day, every network oversight costs hours. The list of what must exist **at the recovery site**, or be
planned in advance:

### Addressing

Two choices, to be made well before the disaster:

- **Keep the same addresses** at the recovery site: nothing to change in the applications, but the network must be
  able to **move** those prefixes, with a BGP advertisement from the other site or
  [stretched VLANs](/en/courses/datacenter/dci#stretching-a-vlan-between-two-sites-why-its-risky), with their risks.
- **Change addresses**: each site has its own clean, routed networks. More robust, but every dependency on an IP
  address (firewall rules, application configurations, licences) must be inventoried and adapted.

### User and customer access

- **DNS**: records to switch to the recovery site's addresses. A short TTL on the names involved, set **in advance**
  ([DNS chapter](/en/courses/fundamentals/dns)), otherwise clients keep the old address for hours.
- **GSLB** or **anycast** address: a [global load balancer](/en/courses/proxy/load-balancing) automatically directs
  to the available site.
- **Internet**: public prefixes advertised in BGP from both sites, the recovery site with a less preferred path (*AS
  path prepend*), so it takes over on its own if the primary disappears.
- **Remote access**: VPN concentrators and remote sites' access (SD-WAN) to the recovery site.

### Infrastructure services

Everything applications need to start must **already be running** at the recovery site: DNS, directory (Active
Directory), [NTP](/en/courses/operations/ntp), DHCP and IPAM, authentication (TACACS+, RADIUS), certificate
authority, monitoring. A restored application that can't find its directory won't start.

### Security

- The recovery site's **firewall rules** are kept up to date at the same pace as the primary's. A gap discovered on
  failover day blocks entire applications.
- **Certificates** are valid for the names and addresses used at the recovery site.

### The network team's tools and access

- [Configuration backups](/en/courses/automation/config-backup), the Git repository, documentation, diagrams:
  **reachable without the primary site**. A repository hosted in the destroyed data center is useless.
- **Break-glass passwords**: the vault must be reachable, or an offline copy available
  ([Vault chapter](/en/courses/automation/vault#the-network-trap-the-vault-during-an-outage)).
- [Out-of-band access](/en/courses/operations/oob) to the recovery site, independent of the primary site.

## The restart order

Applications depend on one another. The DRP describes the restart **order**, from the bottom up:

1. **Network**: core, firewalls, Internet and WAN access;
2. **Infrastructure services**: DNS, directory, NTP, authentication;
3. **Storage**: replication failover, volumes presented;
4. **Databases**;
5. **Applications**, from most to least critical;
6. **User access**: DNS switch, GSLB, BGP advertisements.

Each step has its checks, its owner and a written procedure (*runbook*), followed step by step on the day, by teams
under pressure, maybe in the middle of the night.

## Test, again and again

A DRP that has never been tested doesn't work: forgotten dependencies, missing firewall rules and passwords nobody can
find only show up when you try. Three levels of testing:

| Test | Principle | Indicative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Tabletop** | walk through the scenario in a meeting, procedure in hand, without touching anything | several times a year |
| **Partial** | fail over one application, or one infrastructure service, to the recovery site | regularly |
| **Full** | actually cut the primary site, or simulate it, and run the business from the recovery site | at least once a year |

Each test measures the **RTO and RPO actually achieved**, and ends with a list of fixes. Regulations require it in
some sectors: for finance, the European **DORA** regulation, applicable since 17 January 2025, requires a continuity
policy, recovery plans tested at least once a year, and restores onto systems separate from the original ones.

<Callout type="field">
The test that reveals the most problems: **coming back**. Failing over to the recovery site is prepared; returning to
the primary site, with data that changed in the meantime, rarely is. The DRP must describe both directions.
</Callout>

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom on failover day | Cause |
|---|---|
| Clients keep going to the old site for hours | long DNS TTLs, never lowered in advance |
| Applications start but refuse connections | the recovery site's firewall rules are out of date |
| Nothing starts for lack of directory or DNS | infrastructure services missing from the recovery site |
| The recovery site's data is encrypted too | replication as the only protection, with no isolated or immutable backup |
| The team can find neither the procedure nor the passwords | documentation and vault hosted at the affected site |
| The actual RTO is three times the planned one | plan never tested, restart order unknown |
| Both sites serve at the same time after the link is cut | no witness to break the tie: split-brain |

## Key takeaways

- **BCP**: keep going during the crisis; **DRP**: resume afterwards, within a defined time. The BCP includes the DRP.
- **RTO** (downtime) and **RPO** (data loss) are set by the **business**, after an **impact analysis**, and dictate the
  strategy: from offsite backups to active/active.
- **Replication** also copies errors and ransomware: you need **3-2-1 backups**, including an **offline or immutable**
  copy, tested.
- The network prepares **addressing**, **DNS** (TTL), **BGP** and GSLB, **firewalls**, **infrastructure services**
  and the **team's access** to the recovery site.
- A DRP is **tested**, in both directions, and fixed after every test.
