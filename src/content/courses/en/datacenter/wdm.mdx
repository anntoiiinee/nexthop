Between two racks, you pull as many fibers as needed. Between two buildings, two data centers or two cities, it is
another story: each fiber pair is leased at a high price from a carrier (this is called **dark fiber**, bare fiber with
no equipment), or laid at the cost of civil works. **Wavelength Division Multiplexing** (WDM) multiplies the capacity
of a single pair: instead of one link per fiber pair, several links go through, each on its own **color** of light.

## The principle

A fiber easily carries several wavelengths at the same time, just as a prism splits white light into colors that don't
mix. Each wavelength (written **λ**, lambda) forms an independent **channel**, with its own speed and protocol.

- The **multiplexer** (*mux*) combines the wavelengths of several optics into a single fiber.
- The **demultiplexer** (*demux*) separates them at the far end and sends each one to its port.
- In practice, the same unit does both, for both directions of the fiber pair. Common models are **passive**: simple
  optical filters, with no power or configuration, which therefore don't fail like active equipment.

Each optic plugged into the mux must transmit **exactly** the wavelength of the port it is plugged into: it is called a
**colored optic**. A standard 10GBASE-LR optic, at 1310 nm, doesn't fit.

<Callout type="note">
WDM already exists **inside** some optics. A 100G-CWDM4 or 100GBASE-LR4 optic contains its own multiplexer: four lasers of
different colors combined into a single LC fiber pair (see [Cabling and connectors](/en/courses/datacenter/cabling)).
The WDM of this chapter does the same **outside** the optics, with a separate mux, to share a fiber pair between several
independent links.
</Callout>

## CWDM and DWDM

Two families share the market, depending on channel spacing:

| | CWDM (*Coarse*) | DWDM (*Dense*) |
|---|---|---|
| Standard grid | ITU-T G.694.2: 18 wavelengths from 1271 to 1611 nm, **20 nm** apart | ITU-T G.694.1: a frequency grid around 193.1 THz, spaced **100 or 50 GHz** (about 0.8 or 0.4 nm) |
| Number of channels | up to 18, often 8 or 16 in practice | 40, 80, even more than 90 |
| Lasers | simple, not temperature-controlled, cheap | precise, temperature-stabilized, more expensive |
| Amplification | no | yes, with optical amplifiers (EDFA) in the C band, 1530 to 1565 nm |
| Distance | a few tens of kilometers | hundreds, even thousands of kilometers |
| Typical use | campus, links between nearby data centers, short dark fiber | carriers, long data center interconnects, high capacity |

CWDM wins on **price**: wide channels tolerate imprecise lasers that drift with temperature. DWDM wins on **capacity**
and **distance**: tight channels let you fit many of them, and they all fall in the band where optical amplifiers work,
which regenerates every channel at once.

<Callout type="field">
On older fibers, CWDM channels around 1383 nm are sometimes unusable: the glass of these fibers absorbs strongly at that
wavelength (the "water peak"). Recent single-mode fibers (G.652.D) fixed this defect. Before choosing a CWDM channel plan
on leased fiber, ask the carrier for the fiber type and a loss measurement.
</Callout>

## WDM optics

- **Colored CWDM optics**: SFP, SFP+ or QSFP set at the factory to one wavelength of the CWDM grid. Their latch or label
  often carries a **color code** specific to each wavelength, so they don't get mixed up.
- **Fixed DWDM optics**: set to one channel of the DWDM grid, identified by its number or frequency.
- **Tunable DWDM optics**: the channel is chosen by configuration. A single part number in stock replaces dozens of
  colored optics.
- **Coherent optics** (400ZR, ZR+): 400 Gb/s DWDM optics in QSFP-DD or OSFP form factor, which plug **directly** into a
  switch or router. They use the same modulation technique as carriers' equipment, and the OIF's 400ZR standard targets
  data center interconnects up to about 120 km with amplification. ZR+ variants (OpenZR+ agreement) go further and
  support several speeds.

With pluggable coherent optics, a data center interconnect can do without dedicated transport chassis: the switch
transmits the DWDM wavelength directly, and the mux, often with an amplifier, does the rest. This is called **IP over
DWDM**.

## Multiplexers, add and drop

- **Mux / demux**: ports are labeled by wavelength (1271, 1291…) or DWDM channel, plus a **common** (*line*) port to the
  fiber. Some add a **1310 nm** or **1550 nm** port for a standard optic on top of the colored channels.
- **OADM** (*Optical Add-Drop Multiplexer*): at an intermediate site, it drops and adds a few wavelengths and lets the
  others through. A fiber ring between three sites can thus carry links between each pair of sites.
- **ROADM** (*Reconfigurable OADM*): the remotely controllable version, used by carriers to change dropped wavelengths
  without a site visit.
- **Single-fiber mux**: using different wavelengths for each direction, transmit and receive go through **a single**
  fiber, doubling a pair's capacity.

## The optical budget, again

A mux is passive, but it isn't free: each pass costs power, a few dB depending on the model and number of channels. A WDM
link goes through **two** muxes, on top of the fiber and connectors:

| Element | To add up |
|---|---|
| Single-mode fiber | length × attenuation (about 0.35 dB/km around 1310 nm, less around 1550 nm) |
| Mux at transmit and demux at receive | each one's insertion loss, given by the manufacturer |
| Connectors and patching | a few tenths of a dB each |
| Aging and repair margin | 2 to 3 dB, for future splices and aging |

The total must stay below the **optic's budget** (minimum transmit power minus receiver sensitivity), otherwise you need
more powerful optics or an amplifier.

<Callout type="warning">
The opposite trap exists too: a long-reach optic (ER, ZR) on a short fiber sends **too much** light, and the receiver
saturates. The link is unstable or won't come up, although everything looks perfect. The fix is an optical
**attenuator**, a small part screwed onto the connector that removes a few dB. The optic's datasheet gives the maximum
receive power to respect.
</Callout>

The choices to make:

- **CWDM or DWDM?** At 25 km without amplification, with few channels, CWDM is often enough. If capacity must grow a lot,
  DWDM and its dozens of channels avoid leasing more fiber later.
- **The channel plan**: which wavelength for which link, documented and labeled on every mux port and every optic. Both
  ends of a link use the **same** wavelength, on the same port number of both muxes.
- **Diversity**: two physically separate paths (not in the same trench, not over the same bridge), otherwise a single cut
  takes everything down. Logical redundancy (two BGP links, ECMP) only has value if the fibers are truly independent.
- **Monitoring**: each optic's receive power (DOM), tracked in [monitoring](/en/courses/operations/monitoring), reveals
  a fiber degrading before the cut.

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| One link won't come up, the others do | optic with the wrong wavelength for that mux port, or both ends on different channels |
| No link comes up | transmit and receive swapped on the mux's common port, or wrong fiber type |
| Unstable link over a short distance | receiver saturated by an optic that is too powerful: an attenuator is missing |
| Unstable link over a long distance | budget exceeded: both muxes' losses forgotten, fiber longer than planned |
| All links cut at once | a single physical path, or two paths in the same trench |
| An optic impossible to track down | undocumented channel plan, unlabeled colored optics |

## Key takeaways

- **WDM** carries several links over a single fiber pair, each on its own **wavelength**: a **mux** combines them, a
  **demux** separates them, often passive.
- Each optic must be **colored** to its port's wavelength; both ends of a link use the same one.
- **CWDM**: 18 channels 20 nm apart, cheap, a few tens of km. **DWDM**: dozens of tight channels, amplifiable, for high
  capacity and long distances.
- **Tunable** and **coherent** optics (400ZR) enable IP over DWDM straight from the switch.
- The optical budget includes the loss of **both muxes**; conversely, an optic that is too powerful on a short fiber needs
  an **attenuator**.
- Redundancy relies on **truly separate fiber paths**, and a documented **channel plan**.
