For twenty years, enterprise networks, data centers included, followed the same **three-tier** model:
**access** switches for machines, a **distribution** layer aggregating them, and a **core** in the middle.
That model suited the traffic of the time: users went to servers, and servers to the Internet, from
**north to south**. It suits today's data center much less.

- **Traffic changed direction.** In a modern data center, most traffic goes **server to server**
  (*east-west*): a web application queries a service, which queries a database, which replicates, while
  backups and virtual machine migrations flow. In the three-tier model, that traffic climbs up to
  distribution, or even to the core, and comes back down.
- **Spanning Tree blocks half the links.** Redundant links exist, but carry nothing until a failure (see
  [Spanning Tree](/en/courses/fundamentals/stp)).
- **Latency is not predictable.** Depending on where the two servers sit, the path crosses two, three or
  five devices.
- **Growing is expensive.** Adding capacity means replacing the core with a bigger model.

## The spine/leaf fabric

The spine/leaf architecture (also called a **fabric**, or a **Clos** network, after the engineer who
described it for telephone switches in the 1950s) has only **two tiers**:

- **leaves**: the switches servers plug into, often one or two per rack (*top of rack*);
- **spines**: the switches interconnecting the leaves.

The cabling rules are strict, and that is what makes the model strong:

1. **every leaf connects to every spine**;
2. **leaves do not connect to each other** (except, possibly, a pair of leaves in
   [MLAG](/en/courses/fundamentals/link-aggregation) in the same rack);
3. **spines do not connect to each other**.

Consequences:

- between two servers on two different leaves, there are **always three hops**: leaf, spine, leaf. Latency
  is low and **predictable**;
- between two leaves, there are **as many paths as spines**, all equal-cost, all used at the same time
  thanks to <Term id="ecmp" /> (*Equal-Cost Multi-Path*);
- losing a spine removes a fraction of the capacity (a third with three spines), with no outage.

## Growing horizontally

A fabric grows by **adding** devices, never by replacing them:

- **more server ports**: add leaves, each cabled to every spine;
- **more bandwidth between leaves**: add spines. Each leaf gains an uplink.

The limit is the spines' port count: a 32-port spine accepts at most 32 leaves. Beyond that, you build a
three-stage fabric (*super-spines* above several fabrics), on the same principle.

### Oversubscription

A leaf has many server-facing ports and a few uplinks to the spines. The **oversubscription ratio**
compares both capacities:

| Leaf | To servers | To spines | Oversubscription |
|---|---|---|---|
| 48 × 25 Gb/s + 8 × 100 Gb/s, 4 spines | 48 × 25 = 1,200 Gb/s | 4 × 100 = 400 Gb/s | **3:1** |
| Same leaf, 8 spines | 1,200 Gb/s | 8 × 100 = 800 Gb/s | **1.5:1** |

A 3:1 ratio means that if every server sends at full speed to other leaves, only a third of the traffic
gets through. That is acceptable for most applications, which never all send at once; storage and
distributed computing (AI in particular) aim for 1:1.

## The underlay: a routed fabric

Between leaves and spines, there is **no layer 2 anymore**: each link is a routed link, with a small `/31`
subnet (two addresses, one per end). No Spanning Tree, no possible loop; **routing** chooses the paths and
shares the load.

This basic routed network is called the **underlay**. Its only job: let every leaf reach every other leaf's
**loopback** address, through all spines at once. Server VLANs will be carried **on top**, in the
**overlay** (VXLAN and EVPN chapters).

Several protocols fit (OSPF, IS-IS), but the most widespread in large fabrics is **eBGP** (described in RFC
7938):

- **spines share one AS number**, and **each leaf has its own** (private ASNs, `65000` to `65535`);
- each leaf has an eBGP session with each spine, on the link address;
- each leaf advertises its loopbacks; it receives the other leaves' loopbacks **through each spine**, with
  the same AS path (`65000 65102`), hence the same cost: ECMP does the rest.

Why BGP rather than OSPF? Because it scales to thousands of devices, allows fine-grained policies, and
above all the same protocol will serve the overlay with EVPN: a single protocol to learn and monitor. The
next chapter, [The underlay in depth](/en/courses/datacenter/underlay), comes back to this choice, to the AS
numbering plan, to BFD and to ECMP hashing.

```text title="Arista EOS: leaf1 underlay"
interface Ethernet1
   description spine1
   mtu 9214
   no switchport
   ip address 10.0.1.1/31
!
interface Loopback0
   ip address 10.255.0.11/32
!
router bgp 65101
   router-id 10.255.0.11
   maximum-paths 4 ecmp 4
   neighbor UNDERLAY peer group
   neighbor UNDERLAY remote-as 65000
   neighbor 10.0.1.0 peer group UNDERLAY
   neighbor 10.0.2.0 peer group UNDERLAY
   address-family ipv4
      neighbor UNDERLAY activate
      network 10.255.0.11/32
```

`maximum-paths 4` is essential: without it, BGP installs a single path, and only one spine works.

<Callout type="note">
The **MTU** of fabric links is raised (9214 bytes on Arista, often 9216 elsewhere): the VXLAN overlay adds
50 bytes to every frame, and a server's 1500-byte frame must cross the fabric without fragmentation.
</Callout>

## Special leaves

All leaves look the same, but do not necessarily play the same role:

- **server leaves**: the majority, one or two per rack;
- **border leaves**: the fabric's exit to the rest of the world (campus core, WAN, Internet, another data
  center), often behind firewalls (see [The border leaf](/en/courses/datacenter/border-leaf));
- **service leaves**: shared devices such as firewalls and load balancers, connected to dedicated leaves
  rather than in the middle of the fabric.

## The fabric management network

Each fabric switch has a management port, `Management0` on Arista, placed in a **`MGMT` VRF** (see
[VRFs](/en/courses/datacenter/vrf)). These ports connect to a network **separate** from the fabric: a small
management switch per rack, which also takes the servers' management cards (iDRAC, iLO), and uplinks to a
management core **independent** from the production fabric. That way, an underlay misconfiguration never
cuts off the access to the switches needed to fix it. The [Management and out-of-band
access](/en/courses/operations/oob) chapter details this network, and console access when even it stops
answering.

## In the lab

The `dc-fabric` lab is a fabric with two spines and three leaves, with an eBGP underlay.

<Lab name="dc-fabric" lang="en" />

**1. Underlay sessions.** On leaf1:

```bash
docker exec -it clab-dc-fabric-leaf1 Cli
```

```text
show ip bgp summary
```

Two neighbors, the two spines (`10.0.1.0` and `10.0.2.0`), in *Established* state.

**2. ECMP.** Look at how leaf1 reaches leaf2's VTEP:

```text
show ip route 10.255.1.12
```

The route shows **two next hops**, `10.0.1.0` (spine1) and `10.0.2.0` (spine2): traffic to leaf2 is spread
over both spines. `show ip bgp 10.255.1.12` shows both paths received, with the same AS path `65000 65102`.

**3. Losing a spine.** Shut the link to spine1, then look at the route again:

```text
configure
interface Ethernet1
   shutdown
end
show ip route 10.255.1.12
```

A single next hop remains, through spine2: the fabric keeps working, with half the capacity. A `ping` from
`host1` to `host2` during the cut loses at most a few packets. Bring the interface back (`no shutdown`).

## Key takeaways

- The modern data center is dominated by **east-west** traffic; the three-tier model, with Spanning Tree,
  copes poorly with it.
- The **spine/leaf** fabric: every leaf connected to every spine, no leaf-leaf and no spine-spine links.
  Always **three hops**, and as many paths as spines thanks to **ECMP**.
- Grow **horizontally**: leaves for ports, spines for bandwidth. Watch **oversubscription**.
- The **underlay** is fully routed (`/31` links, raised MTU), most often in **eBGP**: one AS for the spines,
  one AS per leaf, `maximum-paths` for ECMP.
- Fabric management goes through a **separate** network, in a dedicated VRF.
