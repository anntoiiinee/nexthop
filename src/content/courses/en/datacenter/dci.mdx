A company that depends on its IT doesn't rely on a single data center: a fire, a long power outage or a flood, and
everything stops. So it runs **two**, sometimes three, which must be connected: that's **data center
interconnect**, or <Term id="dci" />. The question isn't only "which cable?", but above all "**what** should go
through, and at what cost to resilience?". This chapter answers it, from the physical link to EVPN between sites.

## Why two sites

The [disaster recovery and business continuity](/en/courses/datacenter/pra-pca) chapter set the goals (RTO, RPO,
recovery strategies). On the network side, they translate into two main models:

| Model | Principle | Network requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Active/standby** (disaster recovery) | site B waits, data is replicated to it; it takes over after a failover, within minutes or hours | replicate data, redirect users to B |
| **Active/active** (business continuity) | both sites serve at the same time; losing one site is absorbed by the other | distributed applications, often networks or addresses shared by both sites |

What crosses the interconnect: **routes** (each site reaches the other's networks), sometimes **stretched VLANs**
(the same IP address usable at both sites), and **storage replication**, often the biggest bandwidth consumer.

## The physical link and latency

| Link | Principle | For |
|---|---|---|
| **Dark fiber and WDM** | leased or laid fiber pairs, multiplied by [WDM](/en/courses/datacenter/wdm) | two nearby sites (up to a few tens of km), high capacity, full control |
| **Carrier layer 2 service** | a point-to-point circuit or a wavelength provided by a carrier | when you can't lay your own fiber |
| **Carrier layer 3 service** (MPLS L3VPN) | the carrier routes between the sites | distant sites, layer 3 interconnect only |
| **Internet with IPsec** | an encrypted tunnel over Internet access | backup, small volumes |

**Latency** is set by physics: light travels at about **200,000 km/s** in fiber, i.e. **5 µs per kilometer**. Between
two sites 100 km of fiber apart, a round trip therefore costs at least **1 ms**, not counting the equipment. That's
what limits **synchronous replication** of storage (each write waits for the other site's confirmation) and
application clusters that talk a lot: beyond a few tens of kilometers, you have to go asynchronous.

<Callout type="note">
The fiber's path isn't a straight line: 40 km as the crow flies often means 60 km of fiber, running along roads and
railways. Ask the carrier for the real length, and for resilience, **two physically separate paths**, as in the WDM
chapter's real-world case.
</Callout>

## Layer 3 interconnect: the default choice

The simplest and most robust solution: **each site has its own networks**, and the two sites exchange **routes**
(eBGP between border leaves, or across the carrier's network). An application that fails over from one site to the
other changes IP address, and users follow thanks to DNS, a [global load balancer](/en/courses/proxy/load-balancing)
(GSLB) or an anycast address.

- **A failure stays in its site**: a broadcast storm or a loop at site A doesn't cross the routing.
- **Nothing special to operate**: BGP, route filters, redundant links.
- **The constraint is on the applications**: they must accept a change of address, or sit behind a device that
  hides it.

Modern applications (containers, stateless services behind load balancers, databases replicated by the application)
cope with this very well. VLANs get stretched for the others.

## Stretching a VLAN between two sites: why it's risky

The need is real: live-migrating a virtual machine from one site to the other without changing its address, running
a cluster that shares a virtual IP address, migrating a data center without renumbering everything. The solution seems
obvious: stretch the VLAN. But a VLAN is a **broadcast domain**, and therefore a **failure domain**.

The risks:

- **A single failure domain.** A loop, a broadcast storm, a network card flooding the VLAN: the problem crosses the
  interconnect and hits **both sites at once**. The recovery site goes down with the primary, exactly what it was
  meant to prevent.
- **Split-brain.** If the interconnect is cut, each half of a cluster believes the other is dead, and both declare
  themselves active with the same address. On reconnection, the data has diverged. You need a **witness** (*quorum*)
  at a third site to break the tie.
- **"Tromboning" flows.** A machine at site B whose gateway is at site A sends all its routed traffic across the
  interconnect, and it comes back. Same for a client at site B that enters through site A. Double latency, a
  saturated DCI link.
- **Broadcast traffic over the WAN.** ARP, multicast, unknown frames: all of a VLAN's noise crosses the
  interconnect, which is the most expensive and slowest link.
- **The application isn't built for it.** A web server at site B and its database at site A, "because the VLAN allows
  it": each request pays the inter-site round trip.

<Callout type="warning">
Stretching a VLAN between two sites doesn't make an application resilient: it ties their failures together. Only do
it for a specific need (migration, a cluster that requires it), on **as few VLANs as possible**, with storm
protection, and a plan to remove it once the need is gone.
</Callout>

## Extension technologies

The older methods all have a weak spot:

- **A trunk over dark fiber**, with Spanning Tree or an MLAG on each side (*back-to-back*): simple, but Spanning Tree
  spans both sites, with its convergence and possible loops.
- **VPLS** (a VLAN emulated by an MPLS carrier) and **OTV** (a Cisco proprietary solution, now end-of-life): they
  limited flooding, but with DCI-specific protocols.

Today, the standard answer is **EVPN-VXLAN**, the same technology as inside the fabric
([EVPN chapter](/en/courses/datacenter/evpn)). There are two ways to use it between two sites.

### A stretched fabric

Both sites are treated as **a single fabric**: one EVPN domain, BGP EVPN sessions between all sites, and VXLAN
tunnels directly between any leaves of both sites.

- **Simple**: no specific equipment or configuration.
- **But**: a single control plane for both sites (a configuration error spreads everywhere), tunnels between every
  leaf across the DCI, and all of each site's routes in the other. Acceptable for two very close sites and a small
  fabric.

### Multi-site with gateways

Each site keeps **its own fabric**, its own EVPN domain. At the borders, **gateways** (or *border gateways*) connect
the domains:

The gateway **re-advertises** EVPN routes from one domain to the other **setting itself as the next hop**. Site A's
leaves only see a single VTEP for the whole of site B: their gateway. Traffic follows three successive tunnels (leaf
A to gateway A, gateway A to gateway B, gateway B to leaf B) instead of one. The principle is standardized by RFC 9014;
Cisco calls it *VXLAN EVPN Multi-Site* (with *Border Gateways*), Arista *EVPN Gateway* or multi-domain.

What you gain:

- **Failure containment**: each site has its control plane. The gateway is the only crossing point, where you choose
  **which** VRFs and **which** VLANs go through, and where flooding can be limited.
- **Scale**: no tunnels between every leaf of every site.
- **Independence**: each site can have its own underlay addressing plan, its ASNs, its own evolution.

### On Arista EOS: a layer 3 gateway

Arista's documentation describes a **layer 3 EVPN gateway**: it receives **type 5** routes (IP prefixes) from one
domain and **re-advertises** them into the other, setting itself as the next hop. Here is its example, reduced to
IPv4:

```text title="Arista EOS: gateway between the local fabric and the remote domain (based on Arista's documentation)"
router bgp 65536
   bgp bestpath d-path
   address-family evpn
      neighbor DC_LEAF_PEER_Group activate
      neighbor DCI_REMOTE_GW_Group activate
      neighbor DCI_REMOTE_GW_Group domain remote
      domain identifier 1:1
      domain identifier 2:2 remote
   vrf red
      rd 10.255.1.1:10
      rd evpn domain remote 10.255.1.1:1010
      address-family ipv4
         route-target import evpn 10000:10000
         route-target export evpn 10000:10000
         route-target import evpn domain remote 20000:20000
         route-target export evpn domain remote 20000:20000
      aggregate-address 10.0.0.0/8 summary-only
```

- `neighbor … domain remote`: these neighbors (the other site's gateways, or the WAN) form the **remote** domain; the
  other EVPN neighbors, those of the fabric, form the **local** domain.
- `rd evpn domain remote` and `route-target … domain remote`: the VRF can have a **different** RD and RTs on each
  side. The two sites don't need to agree on their internal values.
- `domain identifier` and `bgp bestpath d-path`: each domain has an identifier, added to the routes crossing it (the
  *D-path*). A route coming back into a domain it already crossed is discarded: that's the loop protection between
  gateways.
- `aggregate-address … summary-only`: the gateway can **summarize** a site's prefixes, and advertise only one to the
  other. Fewer routes, and local instability that doesn't spread.

Extending **VLANs** between domains (type 2 routes, gateway multihoming) also exists, with more configuration: refer
to your vendor's documentation for your version.

## What to extend, and how

- **VRFs first**: with EVPN **type 5** routes (IP prefixes), a VRF exists at both sites and routes from one to the
  other, **without** a shared broadcast domain. That's layer 3 interconnect, carried by EVPN.
- **VLANs next, case by case**: only the VLANs that really need it, listed and justified.
- **ARP suppression** (leaves answer ARP requests themselves thanks to type 2 routes) limits the flooding that
  crosses.
- **Storm control** on the gateways and server ports, so a runaway on one side stays contained.
- **Mobility** is handled by EVPN: when a machine changes site, its MAC is re-advertised with a higher **sequence
  number**, and everyone updates. If a MAC moves too often (two machines with the same address, or a loop), EVPN
  declares it **duplicate** and stops tracking it.

## Optimizing flows between sites

- **Outbound**: the [anycast gateway](/en/courses/datacenter/evpn#the-anycast-gateway) exists on every leaf of
  **both** sites, with the same address and MAC. A machine always leaves through its local leaf, even after changing
  site: no more outbound tromboning.
- **Inbound**: the rest of the Internet or WAN doesn't know which site a machine on a stretched VLAN is in. You
  advertise more specific routes (a /32 route per machine, from the site where it is), or leave the choice to a GSLB.
- **Traffic between the two sites** stays expensive: keep machines that talk a lot together.

## The interconnect itself

- **Redundancy**: two gateways per site, two links over two physically separate paths.
- **MTU**: VXLAN adds 50 bytes to every packet. The DCI link, especially if provided by a carrier, must accept larger
  frames (9,000 bytes if possible), otherwise large packets disappear. The
  [MTU calculator](/en/tools/mtu-calculator) gives the exact count.
- **Encryption**: on a fiber that leaves the building, **MACsec** encrypts at layer 2, link by link, at full speed;
  IPsec is the alternative over a routed network.
- **Fast failure detection**: BFD on the BGP sessions between gateways, to fail over in under a second.

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| A loop at one site takes both down | stretched VLAN without storm protection, or a stretched fabric as a single failure domain |
| After the DCI link is cut, two cluster nodes are active | no witness at a third site: split-brain |
| The DCI link is saturated while the sites are lightly loaded | tromboning flows (gateway or entry at the other site), or tiers of the same application spread over both sites |
| Small packets get through, large ones disappear between sites | DCI link MTU too small for VXLAN |
| A MAC "flaps" between sites then gets blocked | loop, or duplicate address: EVPN declared it duplicate |
| Synchronous replication slows down every write | inter-site round-trip latency: distance too long for synchronous |
| Failover doesn't work on the day | never tested: regularly test losing an entire site |

## Key takeaways

- **DCI** connects two data centers for disaster recovery or business continuity. Light costs **5 µs/km**: 100 km of
  fiber means at least 1 ms round trip.
- **By default, interconnect at layer 3**: each site its own networks, routes exchanged, and a failure that stays in
  its site.
- **Stretching a VLAN ties both sites' failures together**: shared loops and storms, split-brain, tromboning. Only for
  a specific need, on few VLANs, with a witness at a third site.
- **EVPN-VXLAN** is today's technology: as a **stretched fabric** (simple, a single failure domain) or **multi-site**
  with **gateways** that re-advertise routes and contain each site.
- Extend **VRFs** (type 5) first, VLANs case by case; anycast gateway at both sites, an MTU suited to VXLAN, MACsec on
  the fiber, and **tested** failovers.
