Training a large AI model means making **thousands of GPUs** work together for weeks. No GPU can do it all alone:
each computes on part of the data, then all of them **exchange** their results before the next step. Those exchanges
go over the network, and until they are done, the GPUs, which cost tens of thousands of euros each, **wait**. In an AI
cluster, the network is no longer infrastructure: it is part of the computer. This chapter shows how it differs from a
classic fabric, reusing everything the course has laid down: spine/leaf,
[buffers](/en/courses/datacenter/buffers), [QoS, PFC and ECN](/en/courses/datacenter/qos).

## The networks of a GPU cluster

A typical AI server holds **8 GPUs**. It is connected to several separate networks:

| Network | Role | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale-up** | connecting the GPUs **of the same server** (or rack) to each other | proprietary, very high-speed links (NVLink at NVIDIA), not classic Ethernet |
| **Back-end** (*scale-out*) | connecting GPUs **of different servers** for training | one network card per GPU, at 400 or 800 Gb/s; **lossless**, no oversubscription |
| **Storage** | loading training data, writing *checkpoints* | large bursty throughput, often NVMe-oF or parallel file systems |
| **Front-end** | the "normal" network: user access, job scheduler, services | a classic data center fabric |
| **Management** | administration, consoles | the usual [out-of-band network](/en/courses/operations/oob) |

The **back-end** network is what makes an AI cluster unique, and it is what the rest of this chapter describes.

## Why this traffic is different

### Collective communications

At each training step, the GPUs combine their results through **collective operations**, the best known being
**all-reduce**: each GPU contributes its share, and all receive the combined result. Libraries such as NCCL organize
these exchanges, as rings or trees, between all of a job's GPUs.

The decisive consequence: a step is only done when **the last exchange** is done. The network isn't judged on its
average throughput, but on its **slowest flow** (*tail latency*). A single congested link, a single lost packet, and
thousands of GPUs wait. The measure that counts is the **job completion time**.

### Few flows, huge, synchronized

A classic fabric carries millions of small, varied flows, which ECMP spreads well statistically. An AI cluster carries
**few flows**, **huge** ones (*elephant flows* that saturate a 400 Gb/s link), that **all start at the same time**.
Two consequences:

- **ECMP collisions**: ECMP picks a link by **hashing** the headers. With few flows, two elephants often land on the
  same saturated link while a neighboring link stays empty. This is called low **entropy**.
- **Synchronized incast**: every GPU sends at the same moment, creating bursts on the same ports.

## The rail-optimized topology

In a server, GPU 0 mostly talks to the GPU 0s of other servers, GPU 1 to the GPU 1s, and so on (communication
libraries organize exchanges this way). The **rail-optimized** topology takes advantage of it: each **rail** is a
group of leaves to which **the same GPU of every server** is connected.

- The most intense traffic (between GPUs of the same rank) stays **within a rail**, **one hop** away, without going
  through the spines.
- The spines carry exchanges between rails, which are less frequent.
- The fabric is **non-blocking**: as much bandwidth towards the spines as towards the GPUs, with no
  [oversubscription](/en/courses/datacenter/spine-leaf#oversubscription). Any reduction would be paid for in compute
  time.

### Sizing: an example

A cluster of **1,024 GPUs** (128 servers with 8 GPUs), each GPU with a **400 Gb/s** card, and **64-port 400 Gb/s**
switches:

- **8 rails**, one per GPU rank, of **128 GPU ports** each;
- each leaf dedicates **32 ports to GPUs** and **32 ports to spines** (non-blocking): that's **4 leaves per rail**, so
  **32 leaves**;
- the 32 leaves together have **1,024 links** to the spines: with 64-port spines, **16 spines**.

In total, **2,048 links at 400 Gb/s** for the back-end network alone: 1,024 to the GPUs and 1,024 between leaves and
spines, i.e. as many optics or cables. At this scale, [cabling](/en/courses/datacenter/cabling), power and cooling
become topics in their own right.

## RDMA and RoCE

To go fast, GPUs exchange their data with <Term id="rdma" /> (*Remote Direct Memory Access*): the network card reads
and writes **directly** into the remote GPU's memory, without going through the CPU or the system's TCP/IP stack.
Minimal latency, a free CPU.

- **InfiniBand** is RDMA's historical network, designed for high-performance computing.
- **RoCEv2** (*RDMA over Converged Ethernet*) carries RDMA over Ethernet, inside **UDP port 4791**. It is routable,
  so it runs over a classic IP fabric.

RDMA was designed for a **lossless** network: its loss recovery is rudimentary (it retransmits everything from the
lost packet onwards). A 0.1% loss rate is enough to collapse throughput. Hence the recipe we have already seen:

1. **PFC** on the RoCE class, so no packet is dropped through congestion;
2. **ECN** on that same class, with low thresholds, to slow senders down **before** reaching the pauses;
3. **DCQCN**, the congestion control algorithm of RoCE cards: the receiver of an ECN-marked packet sends a
   notification (**CNP**, *Congestion Notification Packet*) back to the sender, which reduces its rate;
4. a **PFC watchdog** everywhere, against pause storms.

<Callout type="warning">
An "almost lossless" RoCE network doesn't exist: a single misconfigured switch, port or card, and the class loses
packets or hangs. Configurations are generated from a model, checked on every change, and PFC pause, ECN marking and
per-queue drop counters are monitored continuously.
</Callout>

## Spreading the load despite the elephants

Against ECMP collisions, several techniques, often combined:

- **More entropy**: multiply RDMA connections (*queue pairs*) between two GPUs; each uses a different UDP source port,
  and therefore a different hash result.
- **Dynamic load balancing** (*dynamic* or *adaptive load balancing*): the switch picks the egress link based on its
  actual load, and can move a flow between two bursts (*flowlets*) without reordering its packets.
- **Packet spraying**: each packet of a flow goes over a different link. The load is perfectly spread, but packets
  arrive **out of order**: you need a network card able to reorder them.
- **Topology**: rail-optimized avoids the spines for most of the traffic.

## InfiniBand or Ethernet?

| | InfiniBand | Ethernet (RoCEv2) |
|---|---|---|
| Lossless | native, through credits (like Fibre Channel) | to be built: PFC, ECN, DCQCN |
| Management | a centralized subnet manager | a classic IP fabric, BGP, EVPN, familiar tools |
| Vendors | essentially one (NVIDIA) | many: switches, cards, optics |
| Skills | specialized | those of network teams |
| Integration | a world apart | the same network as the rest of the data center |

InfiniBand long dominated supercomputers and the first AI clusters. Ethernet is progressing fast, driven by the number
of vendors, available skills and standardization work such as Ultra Ethernet.

## Ultra Ethernet

The **Ultra Ethernet Consortium** (UEC), which brings together switch, card and GPU makers, published its
**specification 1.0 in June 2025**, then version 1.1 in October 2026. It describes a complete stack, from cards and
switches to optics, and above all a new transport, **Ultra Ethernet Transport** (UET, UDP port 4793), designed for AI
and high-performance computing:

- a **modern RDMA** over Ethernet and IP, which accepts packets **out of order**: spraying packets over every path
  becomes possible;
- **selective retransmission** of only the lost packets, instead of resending everything: a loss no longer collapses
  throughput;
- **congestion control** built into the transport.

The goal is to depend less on a lossless network, and therefore on PFC and its risks. Compatible equipment is arriving
gradually; RoCEv2 remains the basis of Ethernet clusters in production today.

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Job completion time varies a lot from day to day | ECMP collisions depending on job placement; static load balancing over few flows |
| RoCE throughput collapses at times | losses in the RDMA class: a device without PFC, or missing ECN letting queues overflow |
| Part of the cluster freezes | PFC pause storm, no watchdog |
| A slow job, underused GPUs | a degraded link or an optic losing packets; a single slow link slows the whole job |
| Checkpoints slow down training | storage network undersized for write bursts |
| Different performance between two rails | swapped cabling (a GPU on the wrong rail), or an oversubscribed rail |

## Key takeaways

- An AI server connects to several networks: **scale-up** (NVLink), **back-end** (GPU to GPU), **storage**,
  **front-end** and management. The back-end is the heart of the cluster.
- **Collective communications** synchronize every GPU: the network is judged on its **slowest flow** and on **job
  completion time**.
- **Rail-optimized**: the same GPU of every server on the same rail, one hop away; a **non-blocking** fabric.
- **RDMA** over Ethernet = **RoCEv2** (UDP 4791), which requires a **lossless** network: PFC, ECN, DCQCN and watchdog.
- Few synchronized **elephant flows**: against ECMP collisions, more entropy, dynamic load balancing, packet spraying.
- **InfiniBand** remains a reference; **Ethernet** is progressing, and **Ultra Ethernet** (specification 1.0 in 2025)
  aims for a transport that tolerates loss and reordering.
