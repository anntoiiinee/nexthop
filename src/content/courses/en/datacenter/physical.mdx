The [Spine/leaf architecture](/en/courses/datacenter/spine-leaf) chapter describes a topology: every leaf connected to
every spine. In the field, you then have to decide **where** those switches physically sit, **how** to connect them to
servers and to each other, with which cables and optics, and how to power them. These choices are made before the
first line of configuration, are expensive to undo, and explain a good share of a data center's "mysterious"
outages. This chapter covers layer 1.

## The room and the racks

Equipment lives in **racks**, standardized cabinets 19 inches wide on the equipment side. Their height is counted in
**units** (U): 1U = 44.45 mm. A standard rack is **42U** (about 2 m), sometimes 45U or 48U. A common server takes 1U
or 2U, a rack switch 1U or 2U, a chassis 7U to 16U. Network racks are often **wider** (800 mm instead of 600 mm) to let
cable bundles run along the sides.

Racks are lined up in **rows**, facing each other, to organize cooling:

- **cold aisles** receive conditioned air, in front of the racks;
- equipment draws air in at the **front** and blows it out hot at the **back**, into the **hot aisles**;
- **containment** (doors and a roof over the cold or hot aisle) keeps both airflows from mixing, and greatly improves
  cooling efficiency.

<Callout type="warning">
A switch's **airflow direction** is chosen when ordering. In a server rack, servers' network cards are almost always
at the **back**, on the hot aisle side: the rack switch is therefore mounted **ports facing the back**, and must draw
air in on the power supply side and blow it out on the port side (*port-side exhaust*). A switch ordered with the
opposite airflow draws in hot air from the hot aisle, heats up, and ends up shutting down or getting damaged. Vendors
sell each model in both directions, with fans and power supplies of different colors: check the part number before
ordering, and the color before installing.
</Callout>

## Where to place access switches

This is the most structuring choice: it determines cable length and count, the number of switches, and the size of a
failure domain. Four models exist.

### Top of Rack (ToR)

Each server rack gets **its own pair of switches**, at the top of the rack. Servers connect with very short cables
that never leave the rack; only the uplinks to the spines, over fiber, run through the cable tray.

It is the natural model of spine/leaf fabrics: **one pair of leaves per rack**, each server connected to both (with
[MLAG](/en/courses/fundamentals/link-aggregation) or EVPN multihoming). A whole rack is installed, cabled and tested as
one block. In return, you need many switches, and in a sparsely filled rack, many of their ports stay unused.

### Top of 3 racks

A ToR variant: the switch pair is installed in the **middle rack** of a group of three, and also serves the racks on
the **left** and **right**. Server cables leave their rack but stay very short, since they only go to the next rack.

The benefit is economic: when racks are limited by power or cooling (a rack of GPU-loaded servers sometimes only
holds a few of them), a single leaf pair finally fills its ports. Cables crossing racks are longer than in pure ToR:
3 to 5 m DACs if they stay short, otherwise AOC or fiber. And the failure domain grows: an incident on the leaf pair
affects three racks.

### Middle of Row and End of Row

Access switches are no longer in server racks, but in a **dedicated network rack**, at the **end** of the row (*End of
Row*, EoR) or in the **middle** (*Middle of Row*, MoR) to shorten the longest cables. They often hold modular
**chassis** with high port counts. Each server rack has a **patch panel**, connected to the network rack by
**structured cabling** laid once and for all.

### Comparing the four models

| | Top of Rack | Top of 3 racks | Middle / End of Row |
|---|---|---|---|
| Switches | one pair per rack | one pair for three racks | a few large switches or chassis per row |
| Server cables | very short, in the rack (DAC) | short, to the next rack (long DAC, AOC) | long, through structured cabling and patching |
| Port utilization | low if racks are sparsely filled | good | very good |
| Failure domain | one rack | three racks | one row |
| Growth | add a complete rack, as a block | add a group of three racks | add line cards to the chassis |
| Typical use | spine/leaf fabrics, cloud, dense racks | power-limited racks (GPU, storage) | enterprise networks, mixed rooms, heterogeneous equipment |

<Callout type="field">
In enterprise data centers, you often see a mix: ToR for recent virtualization rows, legacy EoR in older rooms, and
dedicated network racks for spines, border leaves and firewalls. **Spines**, for their part, are almost always grouped
in one or two network racks, from which fibers run to each leaf.
</Callout>

## Cables

| Type | What it is | Typical reach | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copper RJ45** (Cat6A) | the classic Ethernet cable | 100 m, up to 10 Gb/s | management, servers' administration cards, desktops |
| **DAC** (*Direct Attach Copper*) | copper cable with a module crimped at each end | a few meters (passive), a bit more when active | server to leaf in the same rack: the cheapest and least power-hungry |
| **AOC** (*Active Optical Cable*) | fiber with an optical module crimped at each end | a few tens of meters | neighboring racks, when a DAC is too short or too thick |
| **Multimode fiber** (OM3, OM4, OM5) | wide-core fiber, cheap transmitters | from a few tens to a few hundred meters depending on speed | inside the room: leaf to spine, structured cabling |
| **Single-mode fiber** (OS2) | thin-core fiber, much longer reach | from 500 m to 10 km and beyond | between rooms, between buildings, new 400G+ rooms |

The higher the speed, the shorter the multimode reach: at 10 Gb/s (10GBASE-SR), it reaches 300 m on OM3; at 25 and
100 Gb/s (25GBASE-SR, 100GBASE-SR4), about 70 m on OM3 and 100 m on OM4. That is why new rooms increasingly move to
**single-mode** fiber, even for short distances. These are orders of magnitude: the exact reach is on each optic's
datasheet. The [Cabling and connectors](/en/courses/datacenter/cabling) chapter details fibers, connectors, optics, DACs
and breakout.

### Connectors and structured cabling

- **LC duplex**: two fibers (transmit and receive), for single-lane optics (SR, LR).
- **MPO** (or MTP): 8, 12 or 16 fibers in one connector, for parallel optics (SR4, DR4) and structured cabling
  **trunks**. Wrong **polarity** (transmit on one side landing on transmit on the other) is the classic MPO failure:
  the link doesn't come up, although everything looks properly plugged in.
- **Structured cabling** connects racks with fiber trunks laid once, ending on **patch panels**. Connections are then
  made with short patch cords, without ever pulling a cable out of the raised floor or the cable tray.

<Callout type="tip">
A dirty fiber is the first cause of unstable optical links: an invisible speck of dust on a connector is enough to
drop received power. Clean connectors **before every connection**, with a suitable cleaner, and inspect them with a
fiber scope. Keep protective caps on unused ports and optics.
</Callout>

## Optics and speeds

Switch ports don't take cables directly: you insert pluggable **modules**, whose form factor depends on the speed.

| Form factor | Common speeds | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **SFP+** | 10 Gb/s | the historical server format |
| **SFP28** | 25 Gb/s | today's server standard, SFP+ compatible |
| **QSFP+** | 40 Gb/s | 4 × 10 Gb/s lanes, declining |
| **QSFP28** | 100 Gb/s | 4 × 25 Gb/s lanes; uplinks to spines, spine ports |
| **QSFP-DD** and **OSFP** | 400 and 800 Gb/s | 8 lanes; recent spines, AI fabrics |

A multi-lane port can often be **broken out**: a 100 Gb/s QSFP28 port becomes four 25 Gb/s links, with a breakout
cable (a QSFP28 on one end, four SFP28 on the other). A 400 Gb/s port becomes four 100 Gb/s links. It is a common way
to connect many servers to a switch with fast ports, or to connect different generations. The breakout mode is
configured per port on the switch, depending on the model.

```text title="Checking an optic (Arista EOS and Cisco IOS)"
show interfaces transceiver
show inventory
```

`show interfaces transceiver` shows, for each optic, the temperature and the **transmit and receive power** (DOM,
*Digital Optical Monitoring*). Receive power that is too low points to a dirty, damaged or too long fiber.
`show inventory` lists the modules and their part numbers.

<Callout type="warning">
**Third-party** optics, cheaper than the vendor's, often work well, but some vendors reject them or limit support.
Check the vendor's policy before rolling them out, and don't mix both ends of a link at random: an SR (multimode)
optic doesn't talk to an LR (single-mode) optic, even with the right cable.
</Callout>

## Counting a rack's ports

Before choosing a switch model, count. Example of a rack with 20 virtualization servers:

| Need | Calculation | Ports |
|---|---|---|
| Production NICs, each server connected to both leaves | 20 servers × 2 × 25 Gb/s ports | 40 SFP28 ports, i.e. 20 per leaf |
| Administration cards (iDRAC, iLO) | 20 × 1 copper port | 20 ports on the management switch |
| Uplinks to the spines | 4 spines | 4 QSFP28 ports per leaf |
| Link between the two leaves (MLAG) | 2 links | 2 ports per leaf |

A leaf with 48 SFP28 ports and 8 QSFP28 ports is more than enough, with room for growth, or to serve neighboring racks
in *top of 3 racks*. The ratio between capacity towards servers and towards spines, **oversubscription**, is computed
as explained in the [Spine/leaf architecture](/en/courses/datacenter/spine-leaf) chapter.

## Power and redundancy

- Each rack receives **two independent power feeds** (A and B), each with its own **PDU** (the rack's power strip).
- Each switch and server has **two power supplies**, one on each feed: losing one feed cuts nothing.
- Each server is connected to **both leaves** of its pair; the two leaves must not depend on the same power feed or the
  same cable path.
- The **power** available per rack (often a few kilowatts to a few tens) often limits the number of servers well
  before the number of units: that is what pushes towards *top of 3 racks* for very dense racks.

## The management network

On top of the fabric, each rack has a small **management switch** for switches' management ports and servers'
administration cards, and network devices have **console** access through a console server. This network is
separate from production, to keep control of devices when the fabric is down: that is the topic of the
[Management and out-of-band access](/en/courses/operations/oob) chapter.

## Documenting the physical layer

A well-cabled but poorly documented rack quickly becomes a poorly cabled rack. Three rules:

- **every cable is labeled at both ends**, with a unique identifier, and the same identifier is in the documentation
  tool. Cabling suppliers deliver patch cords, DACs and trunks **labeled at the factory**, with the text of your choice
  printed at each end (identifier, rack and port of each end, sometimes a barcode): you provide the cable list with the
  order, and labeling no longer depends on everyone's care during installation;
- **rack elevations** and **cables** are kept in [NetBox](/en/courses/automation/netbox), which can represent racks,
  each device's unit, patch panels and the full path of a connection;
- interface names and descriptions follow the [naming conventions](/en/courses/automation/naming-ipam), which lets you
  automatically compare planned cabling with what LLDP sees.

## Classic pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Switch running hot, temperature alarm, shutdown | airflow ordered in the wrong direction for the rack |
| Optical link doesn't come up | wrong MPO polarity, SR and LR optics mixed, multimode and single-mode fiber mixed |
| Unstable link, CRC errors | dirty connector, fiber bent too tightly, DAC too long for the speed |
| Optic rejected by the switch | unsupported third-party optic, or incompatible with the port mode |
| A whole rack lost during power maintenance | both leaves' power supplies on the same feed |
| Impossible to find where a cable goes | missing labels or outdated documentation |

## Key takeaways

- Equipment lives in **racks** measured in **U**, lined up in **hot and cold aisles**; a switch's **airflow direction**
  is chosen when ordering, and getting it wrong causes overheating.
- **Top of rack**: one pair of leaves per rack, short cables, the fabric model. **Top of 3 racks**: one pair in the
  middle rack for three racks, better port use. **Middle / End of row**: switches grouped, structured cabling.
- Cables: **DAC** in the rack, **AOC** to neighbors, **fiber** multimode or single-mode beyond; multimode reach drops
  as speed rises.
- **SFP28** optics on the server side, **QSFP28** to **OSFP** on the spine side; **breakout** turns a fast port into
  several links.
- Two **power feeds**, two power supplies, two leaves per server, and a separate **management network**.
- Label every cable at both ends, and document racks and cables in **NetBox**.
