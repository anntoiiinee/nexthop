Building a fabric takes a few weeks. Running it lasts for years: software upgrades, new leaves, optic replacements,
new VLANs every week, and one day, a failure to understand quickly. The previous chapters drew an architecture that is
**redundant everywhere**: several spines, servers connected to two leaves, border leaves in pairs. That redundancy is
not only there to survive failures; above all, it lets you **work on any device without interrupting service**,
provided you go about it in the right order.

This last chapter brings together the operational routines: taking a device out of the traffic before touching it,
upgrading a whole fabric switch by switch, checking automatically that everything came back as before, and knowing
what to monitor day to day.

## Never touch a device that carries traffic

The golden rule of fabric maintenance: before rebooting a switch, **remove the traffic** going through it, **check**
that nothing goes through anymore, and only then intervene. Rebooting a spine "hot" often works (BGP and BFD detect
the failure, ECMP fails over), but every flow crossing it suffers an interruption, and a reboot that goes wrong leaves
a half-alive device in the middle of the fabric.

Removing the traffic is called **draining**. The idea: make the routes through the device **less attractive** than
the others, so that its neighbors move to the other paths on their own, with no loss, before anything is shut down.

### The BGP drain

In an eBGP fabric, the most portable technique is **AS-path prepending**: the spine to drain adds its AS several
times to the routes it advertises.

Sessions stay established, routes stay known: if spine2 failed during the maintenance, spine1 would take the traffic
back at once. This is a **drain**, not a shutdown.

```text title="Arista EOS: draining spine1"
route-map DRAIN permit 10
   set as-path prepend 65000 65000 65000
!
router bgp 65000
   neighbor UNDERLAY route-map DRAIN out
```

To put the spine back into service (*undrain*), remove the route-map. Other methods exist: the **GRACEFUL_SHUTDOWN**
community (RFC 8326), which neighbors translate into a zero local preference (effective with iBGP or when neighbors
are configured to honor it), or lowering the MED. AS-path prepending has the advantage of working everywhere, with
nothing to configure on the neighbors.

### Maintenance mode

Vendors have built this drain into a single command, which can also drain other protocols and check that traffic
has actually left the device:

```text title="Arista EOS: putting the whole switch into maintenance"
maintenance
   unit System
      quiesce
```

```text title="Cisco NX-OS: Graceful Insertion and Removal"
system mode maintenance
```

`show maintenance` (Arista) and `show system mode` (Cisco) track the state: *maintenance mode entering*, then *under
maintenance* when the drain is complete. On exit (`no quiesce`, or `no system mode maintenance`), the switch
advertises its routes normally again. The benefit: one identical procedure for every device, harder to get wrong than
a route-map typed by hand.

### Draining a leaf

A spine is drained through BGP. A leaf, however, carries servers: first, **its servers** must leave it.

1. **Shut the links to dual-homed servers** (or put them in maintenance): LACP removes those links from the bonds,
   and the servers carry on through the other leaf. With ESI-LAG, the leaf withdraws its type 1 routes, and the whole
   fabric fails over (see [EVPN multihoming](/en/courses/datacenter/multihoming)); with MLAG, the peer takes over.
2. **Drain the underlay** (maintenance mode): nothing transits through it anymore.
3. **Check** the counters: no more traffic on the server ports or the uplinks.
4. **Intervene.**

Servers connected to **a single** leaf will suffer an outage, whatever you do. This is the most concrete argument for
dual-homing: without it, every leaf upgrade becomes a maintenance window negotiation with the application teams.

## The rolling upgrade

Upgrading a whole fabric means chaining this cycle **one device at a time** (*rolling upgrade*), starting with the
least risky:

The usual order:

1. **one spine**, then the others one by one: the fabric loses a fraction of its capacity during each intervention,
   never connectivity;
2. **the leaves**, one pair at a time, and within each pair one leaf then the other;
3. **the border leaves** last, one by one, during a low-traffic window: north-south traffic goes through them.

A few rules that avoid nasty surprises:

- **a single target version**, validated in a lab or on a first, less critical device; read the release notes,
  especially **known bugs** and **changes in default behavior**;
- **never two redundant devices at once**: not two spines, not both leaves of a pair;
- **do not stay between two versions for long**: a fabric where two versions coexist for months eventually hits a
  case nobody tested;
- **a way back**: the previous image stays on the switch, and you know how to put it back in `boot system`.

Some platforms can restart their software while keeping the data plane running (Arista SSU, Cisco ISSU): the outage
for single-homed servers drops below a second. It is useful, but it does not replace the drain: use it only where
dual-homing is impossible.

## Validating with ANTA

After each intervention, a simple question needs an answer: **is the fabric back exactly in the expected state?**
Checking it by hand, switch by switch, with `show` commands, takes time, and something always gets forgotten.
<Term id="anta" /> (*Arista Network Test Automation*) automates this check: a **catalog** describes the expected
state as tests, an **inventory** lists the devices, and ANTA runs every test on every device in a few seconds.

```yaml title="catalog.yml (lab excerpt)"
anta.tests.routing.bgp:
  # All underlay (IPv4) and overlay (EVPN) sessions are established
  - VerifyBGPPeersHealth:
      address_families:
        - afi: ipv4
          safi: unicast
          vrf: default
        - afi: evpn

anta.tests.routing.generic:
  # Every device reaches the three VTEPs
  - VerifyRoutingTableEntry:
      vrf: default
      routes:
        - 10.255.1.11
        - 10.255.1.12
        - 10.255.1.13
```

Run the same catalog **before** the intervention (to make sure you start from a healthy state, and not get blamed for
a pre-existing failure) and **after** (to prove you put everything back). A red test after the upgrade stops the
procedure: you do not move on to the next device.

The catalog is best **generated** rather than written: AVD knows every link, every BGP session and every VTEP of the
fabric, and produces the matching ANTA catalog (see [Arista AVD](/en/courses/automation/avd)). The expected state then
comes from the same source as the configuration itself.

## What to monitor

ANTA checks the state at a given moment, on demand. Monitoring watches all the time. In a fabric, the indicators that
matter:

| What | Why | Typical alert |
|---|---|---|
| **BGP sessions** (underlay and EVPN), and number of received prefixes | A dropped session means one path or neighbor fewer; a falling prefix count, a lost advertisement | Session not *Established*; received prefixes down by more than 10% |
| **BFD sessions** | A flapping session flags an unstable link before it fails | Several state changes within an hour |
| Interface **errors, discards and optics** | Silent failures start with CRC errors or dropping optical power | Rising errors; receive power close to the low threshold |
| **Uplink balance** | A spine noticeably busier than the others reveals an ECMP problem or elephants | Persistent load gap between one leaf's uplinks |
| **Hardware table usage** (MAC, ARP, routes) | A full table does not announce itself: new entries are simply ignored | Usage above 80% |
| **MAC mobility** | A MAC constantly changing leaf signals a loop or a duplicate address | EVPN duplicate MAC detection |
| **Buffer discards and PFC pauses** | Output drops or pause storms (see [Switch buffers](/en/courses/datacenter/buffers)) | Rising output discards; PFC watchdog triggered |
| **NTP synchronization** | Without a common time, correlating five switches' logs during an outage is impossible | Offset above a few milliseconds |
| **Configuration drift** | A change made by hand, outside the GitOps flow, will be overwritten by the next deployment | Running configuration differs from the intent |

These metrics are best collected through **telemetry** (gNMI, continuously, every few seconds) rather than by polling
switches every five minutes over SNMP: a BFD micro-outage or a burst of discards leaves no trace in a five-minute
average. The [Services and observability](/en/courses/operations) course details the whole chain:
[SNMP, syslog and NetFlow](/en/courses/operations/monitoring),
[Telemetry with gNMI and gNMIc](/en/courses/operations/telemetry),
[Prometheus and Grafana](/en/courses/operations/prometheus-grafana), [NTP](/en/courses/operations/ntp), and the
[out-of-band network](/en/courses/operations/oob) that lets you reach a switch when the fabric itself is down.

<Callout type="field">
The best monitoring does not replace a **written** and rehearsed **procedure**. The longest incidents do not come from
hardware failures, but from interventions done in the wrong order: both leaves of a pair upgraded at the same time, a
spine rebooted without a drain while the other was already in maintenance, a border leaf left in maintenance after the
window. Maintenance mode, ANTA before and after, and a pipeline that chains the steps make those mistakes much harder.
</Callout>

## The bridge to automation

This whole chapter converges on one idea: **a fabric is operated as code**.

- **The intent** (VLANs, VRFs, leaves, sessions) lives in a data model, versioned in Git;
- **the configurations** and the **test catalog** are generated from it by AVD;
- **every change** goes through a merge request: diff reviewed, deployment, then ANTA tests (see
  [Arista AVD](/en/courses/automation/avd));
- **backups** of the actual configuration detect any drift (see
  [GitOps configuration backups](/en/courses/automation/config-backup));
- **upgrades** follow the same logic: a pipeline that drains, upgrades, tests and puts back into service, one device
  after another, and stops at the first red test.

A fabric operated this way is not only faster to evolve: it is **predictable**, and that is what you expect from an
infrastructure every application of the company relies on.

## In the lab

The `dc-fabric` lab puts the switches on a management network, `172.20.40.0/24`, with eAPI enabled: that is how ANTA
queries them. The lab's `anta/` folder holds the inventory and the catalog.

<Lab name="dc-fabric" lang="en" />

**1. Pre-checks.** On your machine, in a Python virtual environment, install ANTA, then run the catalog from the lab
folder:

```bash
pip install "anta[cli]"
anta nrfu -u admin -p admin -i anta/inventory.yml -c anta/catalog.yml table
```

A table, one row per test and per device. Everything should be green (*success*), except perhaps
`VerifyRunningConfigDiffs`: it fails on a switch whose configuration was changed without being saved
(`write memory`), for example during the previous chapters.

**2. Drain spine1.** Start a continuous ping from `host1` to `host3`, in another terminal:

```bash
docker exec -it clab-dc-fabric-host1 ping 10.10.20.13
```

On leaf1, note the two next hops towards leaf3's VTEP:

```text
show ip route 10.255.1.13
```

Then, on spine1, apply the drain:

```text
configure
route-map DRAIN permit 10
   set as-path prepend 65000 65000 65000
router bgp 65000
   neighbor UNDERLAY route-map DRAIN out
```

**3. Check the drain.** On leaf1, `show ip route 10.255.1.13` now shows a single next hop, through spine2.
`show ip bgp 10.255.1.13` still shows both paths, but spine1's, longer (`65000 65000 65000 65000 65103`), is no longer
selected. The ping lost **no** packets. On spine1, `show interfaces counters rates` shows the links to the leaves
almost idle: only BGP and BFD packets still go through.

**4. During the maintenance.** Run ANTA again: `VerifyBGPPeersHealth` stays green (sessions are still established),
`VerifyRunningConfigDiffs` flags spine1's unsaved change, and an actual reboot of spine1 would turn the BGP tests red
for spine1 and for every leaf. That is expected: during an intervention, compare the report with what you expect from
the intervention.

**5. Back into service.** On spine1:

```text
configure
router bgp 65000
   no neighbor UNDERLAY route-map DRAIN out
   exit
no route-map DRAIN
```

On leaf1, both next hops come back. Run ANTA again: everything is green, as before. The intervention is over, and you
have proof that it left nothing behind.

## Key takeaways

- Never touch a device that carries traffic: **drain** it (AS-path prepend, GRACEFUL_SHUTDOWN community, or
  **maintenance mode**), **check**, then intervene.
- A leaf is drained by first moving its **dual-homed servers** away; single-homed servers always suffer an outage.
- **Rolling upgrade**: spines one by one, then leaves pair by pair, border leaves last; never two redundant devices at
  once.
- **ANTA** checks the expected state of the whole fabric in seconds: **before** and **after** every intervention; its
  catalog is generated with AVD.
- Monitor: BGP and BFD sessions, errors and optics, ECMP balance, hardware tables, MAC mobility, buffers, NTP and
  configuration drift, preferably through **telemetry**.
- A fabric is operated **as code**: intent in Git, configurations and tests generated, changes reviewed, deployed and
  validated by a pipeline.
